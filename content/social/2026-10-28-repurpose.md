## 1) X Thread (10 Tweets)

1/ Every month, Madison Chen makes $5,200 but feels broke by the 20th. The problem? She has no idea where it goes. Takeout, Amazon impulse buys, forgotten subscriptions. Sound familiar? Zero-based budgeting might be the antidote. Here's how it works 🧵

2/ Zero-based budgeting is simple: Income minus expenses equals ZERO. Not zero in your bank account—zero unassigned dollars in your budget. Every single dollar gets a job before you spend it. No vague "miscellaneous" buckets. No guilt. Just clarity.

3/ Madison's $5,200 paycheck breaks down like this: $1,400 rent, $600 loans, $500 groceries, $300 Roth IRA, $400 dining out, $200 emergency fund, $150 sinking fund, $200 fun money... and so on. Every dollar accounted for. Total: $5,200. Remaining: $0.

4/ Unlike percentage-based budgets (50/30/20), ZBB forces intentionality. You decide before spending, not after. When Madison's $400 dining budget hits $40 on the 18th, she knows exactly what she can do—no guessing, no guilt trips.

5/ The psychological shift is real: ZBB grants *permission* to spend money you've allocated. Need $60 for art supplies from your $100 hobby budget? That's not a guilt purchase—that's literally what that money is for. Budget says yes.

6/ Building yours? Start with actual take-home pay (after taxes, insurance, deductions). Then list every expense—fixed, variable, irregular, and discretionary. Split vague categories. "Food" → "groceries" + "restaurants." "Shopping" → "clothing" + "books."

7/ Assign dollar amounts until you hit zero. This month you have $5,200 to allocate. Next month, rebuild from scratch. Utilities spike in summer? Your budget reflects it. Friend's wedding in June? You allocate for it. It's not autopilot—it's intentional every month.

8/ Track relentlessly. Use YNAB, EveryDollar, or a spreadsheet. Log every purchase within 24 hours. If restaurants hit budget halfway through, adjust: steal from a category you're not using. The total still equals your income, but the allocation matches reality.

9/ Three things that kill ZBB: variable income (budget conservatively—use your lowest 6-month average), unexpected expenses (build an "emergency buffer" category), and social friction (tell friends you're budgeting; suggest cheaper hangouts like park picnics).

10/ The payoff? No more overdrafts on the 20th. No mystery about where money went. Just intentional spending and actual savings growth. Madison finally builds that emergency fund. ZBB isn't sexy, but it works—and weirdly, people say it's kinda fun. Try it next month.
