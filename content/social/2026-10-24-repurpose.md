# Repurposed Content

## 1) X Thread (10 Tweets Max)

1/ Debt consolidation sounds magical: one payment, lower interest, done in 5 years. But it's a trap if the math doesn't work or you haven't fixed your spending habits. Here's when it actually helps (and when it wrecks you). 🧵

2/ The promise: combine 5 credit cards into 1 loan. The reality: consolidation only saves money if you qualify for a meaningfully lower interest rate—at least 3-5 points lower than your weighted average. Bad credit? You might pay MORE, not less.

3/ Real math: Jordan has $22k across 4 cards at 23.3% weighted APR, paying $690/month in minimums. A consolidation loan at 12.5% = $493/month and saves $10,820 in interest. But that only works because her credit score qualified her for a better rate.

4/ Here's the #1 reason consolidation backfires: you pay off credit cards, see available credit, and start spending again. Now you're carrying both the consolidation loan AND new credit card debt. Worse off than before. This is the trap.

5/ Before consolidating, ask yourself: Have I fixed the spending habits that created this debt? If the answer is "not really," consolidation is a band-aid. You need a budget overhaul first, then consider the strategy.

6/ The credit score hit is real but temporary. Hard inquiry drops your score ~5-10 points. But moving card balances to a personal loan can slash your utilization ratio from 75% to 0%, often raising your score within 2-3 months. Short pain, medium-term gain.

7/ Warning: Don't consolidate credit card debt with a home equity loan unless you're 100% sure you won't miss payments. You're converting unsecured debt into secured debt backed by your house. Default = foreclosure risk.

8/ Balance transfer cards (0% APR for 12-21 months) can work if you have discipline. But that 3-5% upfront fee eats into savings. And if you miss the deadline, the retroactive interest is brutal.

9/ The bottom line: Consolidation works when (1) math shows real savings, (2) your credit score qualifies you for better rates, (3) you've genuinely fixed your spending, and (4) you can reliably make the new payment for the full term. Miss any of these? Stay away.

10/ Still considering it? Use a calculator to model your actual scenario. Compare total interest paid across all methods. Then ask yourself the hard question: Will I actually change my habits, or am I just rearranging deck chairs?
