## 1) X Thread (10 Tweets Max)

```
1/ Debt consolidation sounds perfect: one loan, one payment, financial peace. But the math doesn't always match the marketing.

A personal loan at 14.39% APR might save you thousands—or trap you in a costlier, longer repayment. Here's what actually works.

2/ The sweet spot: you qualify for a lower rate, you stop accumulating new debt, and the monthly payment fits your budget.

Example: Sarah has $18K across 3 credit cards at ~22% APR. A personal loan at 12.5% saves her $1,960 in interest and 2 years of payments.

3/ The math only works if your new APR is 3–5 points lower than your weighted average. Calculate it before applying.

Borrowers with scores 700+ can access reasonable rates. Below 650? You're looking at 20–36% APR—barely better than credit cards.

4/ ⚠️ The biggest trap: stretching the loan term to lower monthly payments.

A 7-year loan might cost MORE in total interest than a 3-year loan, even at a lower rate. Run both scenarios. Always.

5/ Watch for origination fees (1–6% of loan amount). A $20K loan + 5% fee = +$1K cost. If your interest savings total $900, you lost money.

6/ Don't consolidate low-rate debt into higher-rate debt.

Federal student loans (5.5–8%), auto loans (6%–) should stay separate. Only consolidate high-interest obligations.

7/ The rebound trap: You consolidate, your credit score improves, you reopen those credit cards... and spend again.

Now you have a loan + fresh credit card debt. One problem became two.

8/ Medical debt is different. Hospital payment plans are often 0% interest. Consolidating them into a 12% loan makes it MORE expensive.

Explore charity programs, payment plans, or state relief before borrowing.

9/ Not sure consolidation is right? Consider:
• Balance transfer cards (0% for 12–21 months, good credit required)
• Debt management plans (nonprofits negotiate lower rates, no new loan)
• Home equity loans (if you're a homeowner, lower rates but risky)

10/ Bottom line: Consolidation works when the new rate beats the old ones AND you commit to not borrowing again.

It's not a financial reset—it's refinancing. Make sure the math actually works.
```
