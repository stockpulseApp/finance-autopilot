## 1) X Thread (10 tweets max)

1/ Stop guessing at freelance rates. Most undercharge because they've never calculated what they actually *need* to earn. Your gut isn't a business plan. Here's the math.

2/ Start with your target take-home income. Let's say $75k/year. Add ~27% for taxes + business expenses (software, insurance, platform fees). You're now at $112k+ in revenue you need to generate. That's your floor—not your asking price.

3/ Now divide by billable hours: 48 weeks × 30 billable hours/week = 1,440 hours. $112k ÷ 1,440 = $78/hour minimum. Below that? You're subsidizing clients with your own financial security.

4/ But hourly rates reward speed and scare away clients. Better approach: project-based pricing anchored to *client value*, not your effort. A $60k problem you solve is worth more than 40 hours of your time.

5/ Structure a pricing menu: Starter (defined, low-touch), Standard (2.5–3× starter), Premium (deliberately expensive). Clients pick the middle. The premium tier makes standard look reasonable.

6/ A Chicago illustrator tested this: stopped asking "How many hours?" Started asking "Is it worth it?" Her hourly equivalent jumped from $35 to $125+ by pricing outcomes, not effort.

7/ In your proposal, never lead with credentials or close with a number. Lead with their problem. Quantify the cost of inaction. Position your price as a fraction of the value you'll create.

8/ When they say "too expensive"—don't apologize or discount immediately. Ask: "What would make this work?" Often they need a payment plan or a stripped-down scope. Find the friction.

9/ Market rates aren't intuitive. They're researched. A designer charged ₹7k/flyer for years until discovering peers charged ₹10k+. He "just didn't know better." Run the comps.

10/ You're probably worth more than you're charging. Prove it with math, not feelings. Calculate your floor. Research your market. Package it in tiers. Defend the number. Move forward.
