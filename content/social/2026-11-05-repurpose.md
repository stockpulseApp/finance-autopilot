# Social Security Timing: Repurposed Content

## 1. X THREAD (10 tweets)

1/ You're facing a six-figure decision disguised as a simple birthday: when to claim Social Security. The choice between 62, 67, or 70 can create $100K+ differences. Most people never run the numbers. Here's what the data actually says. 🧵

2/ Your full retirement age (FRA) is the inflection point. Born 1960+? Your FRA is 67. Claim at 62 and you lose 30% permanently. Wait until 70 and you gain 8% per year. That's the lever that changes everything.

3/ On a $2,000 monthly benefit at FRA:
- Claim at 62: $1,400/month
- Claim at 67: $2,000/month
- Claim at 70: $2,480/month

$1,080/month gap = $13K/year difference, compounding for life. The breakeven math most people use misses this.

4/ The crossover point (where delay catches up) is usually 78-81. But that's incomplete math. COLA adjustments compound differently on larger benefits. An 8% raise on $2,480 adds $198/month vs $112 on $1,400. Over decades, that gap explodes.

5/ You're also ignoring tax implications. Up to 85% of Social Security can be taxed if your combined income exceeds $25K (single) or $32K (married). A bigger delayed benefit might push more into taxable territory—or reduce your need to tap IRAs early.

6/ The spousal/survivor angle changes everything. Higher earners who delay maximize the survivor benefit—potentially for decades. If you're the primary earner, that $240K difference might protect your spouse for 20+ years after you're gone.

7/ Case study: Maria, age 61, has $380K saved and projects $2,400/month at 67. Delay to 70? She'd withdraw $72K more from savings to bridge the gap. At 5% returns, that opportunity cost ($155K by age 88) nearly erases the delay benefit.

8/ The Medicare trap: Claim Social Security before 65 = auto-enrollment in Medicare at 65 with Part B premiums deducted from your check. Delay past 65 without employer coverage? You might miss enrollment and face 10% permanent premium penalties. Plan accordingly.

9/ When early claiming wins: You have health concerns, no other income, aren't working, or need money now. The "wait until 70" narrative breaks down if you expect to live to 78, not 95. Health data beats philosophy.

10/ Bottom line: Run the breakeven analysis, yes. But also map spousal strategies, Medicare timing, and your actual longevity markers. The "right" answer is different for everyone—and most calculators ignore half the variables that matter.
