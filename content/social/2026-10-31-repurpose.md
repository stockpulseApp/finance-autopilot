## 1) X Thread (10 tweets max)

1/ The median first-time homebuyer is now 40 years old—an all-time high. High rates + soaring prices = homeownership feels impossible. But there's a workaround: house hacking. Here's how it works 🧵

2/ House hacking: Buy a property, live in one unit, rent out the rest. Let tenants cover your mortgage. You build equity while your housing costs stay low (or disappear entirely).

3/ The game-changer? Owner-occupied financing. FHA loans let you buy up to 4-unit properties with just 3.5% down—way better rates than investor mortgages. That's the leverage that makes house hacking viable.

4/ Real math: Mara buys a $360K duplex in Columbus with FHA. Down payment: $12,600. She lives in one side, rents the other for $1,650/month. Her out-of-pocket cost: $1,482/month. (Her old rent was $1,400.)

5/ The difference? She's now building equity instead of padding her landlord's pockets. Plus tax deductions on depreciation, mortgage interest, and property taxes.

6/ After year one, she can move out and rent both units. Suddenly collecting $3,300/month while her mortgage stays locked at $2,932. That duplex becomes a cash-flowing asset.

7/ But here's the catch: You need $15K–$25K in cash upfront (down payment + closing costs + reserves). Saving that is often harder than qualifying for the loan.

8/ House hacking isn't glamorous. You're a landlord. You share walls with tenants. It's wealth-building, not lifestyle perfection. It works best if you're willing to sacrifice privacy for financial progress.

9/ Is it worth it? For first-time buyers priced out of traditional homeownership—yes. For people who value solo living—probably not. Know which camp you're in before you commit.

10/ Bottom line: House hacking isn't a get-rich-quick scheme. It's a legitimate wealth-building strategy for buyers willing to house hack for 1–3 years, then scale into a rental portfolio.
