# House Hacking Explained: How First-Time Buyers Can Live for Free (Almost)

The rent on Mia Chen's Capitol Hill apartment hit $2,100 in late 2024. She was 28, earning $72,000 as a project coordinator, and watching half her take-home pay vanish into housing. Then a friend mentioned house hacking—buying a duplex, living in one unit, renting the other—and the math suddenly made sense.

Six months later, Chen closed on a $385,000 duplex in Indianapolis with a 3.5% FHA loan. Her tenant pays $1,400 a month. After her mortgage, taxes, and insurance run about $2,650, she's living in her own unit for roughly $1,250 monthly—and building equity instead of burning cash on rent.

House hacking isn't new, but it's having a moment. With median rents near all-time highs and single-family homes priced out of reach for many millennials and Gen Z buyers, this live-in landlord strategy offers a rare on-ramp to ownership that doesn't require a trust fund or a six-figure salary.

Here's what you need to know before you jump in—including where it actually pencils out, how to finance it, and the unglamorous realities of sharing a property line with your tenants.

## What house hacking actually means

House hacking is the practice of purchasing a property with multiple rentable spaces, occupying one, and leasing the others to offset your housing costs. The most common structures include:

- **Duplex, triplex, or fourplex**: You live in one unit and rent the rest. This is the classic house-hack setup. [CrossCountry Mortgage](https://crosscountrymortgage.com/mortgage/resources/what-is-house-hacking) notes that because you occupy the property as your primary residence, you can access owner-occupant financing programs unavailable to pure investors.

- **Single-family home with rentable rooms**: You lease spare bedrooms to housemates. Less capital-intensive but more intimate—you're sharing a kitchen, not just a driveway.

- **ADU (accessory dwelling unit) configuration**: You own a property with a detached garage apartment, basement suite, or backyard cottage and rent that space. [Realtor.com highlighted five properties under $500K with renter-ready ADUs](https://www.realtor.com/news/unique-homes/bargain-houses-adu-guest-suites-rent-income-money), showing this niche is growing as zoning laws relax in cities desperate for housing supply.

The unifying logic: rental income from tenants covers part—or in ideal scenarios, all—of your mortgage, property taxes, insurance, and maintenance. You're essentially house-sitting your own investment while someone else foots the bill.

[Johnson Financial Group](https://www.johnsonfinancialgroup.com/resources/blogs/buying-or-building-a-home/what-is-house-hacking-the-real-estate-strategy-that-pays-toward-your-mortgage) frames it well: house hacking works best for buyers who "prioritize wealth-building over lifestyle perfection" and are "comfortable with delayed gratification." Translation: you sacrifice some privacy and convenience now for a step-change in net worth later.

## The financing advantage: how lenders make it possible

The killer feature of house hacking is leverage. Because you'll live on-site, lenders treat the purchase as an owner-occupied primary residence—not an investment property. That distinction unlocks:

**FHA loans**: As little as 3.5% down if your credit score is 580 or higher. On a $400,000 fourplex, that's $14,000 instead of the 20–25% ($80,000–$100,000) typically required for a non-owner-occupied rental. [Lower Mortgage](https://www.lower.com/mortgages/what-is-house-hacking) confirms FHA financing works for properties with up to four units, provided you occupy one.

**Conventional loans with lower down payments**: Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac offer programs requiring 5–10% down for two- to four-unit properties when you're the primary resident.

**Better interest rates**: Owner-occupant loans come with rates roughly 0.5–0.75 percentage points lower than investor loans. On a 30-year mortgage, that can mean tens of thousands in savings.

There's a catch: most lenders require you to live in the property for at least 12 months. Violate that occupancy clause and you could be in breach of your loan agreement. After year one, though, you're free to move out, convert the entire building to rentals, and even house-hack a second property.

## Where the numbers actually work in 2026

House hacking is location-sensitive. In coastal metros where a duplex costs $1.2 million, rental income rarely covers the mortgage; in the Midwest, the arbitrage can be dramatic.

[Mortgage Professional America reported that Indianapolis, Indiana, claimed the top spot](https://www.mpamag.com/us/mortgage-industry/market-updates/the-cities-where-house-hacking-actually-pencils-out-in-2026/586218) in 2026 house-hacking rankings, driven by strong rent-to-price ratios. Other Midwest and Sun Belt cities where the strategy pencils include:

- **Cleveland, Ohio**: Duplexes in Tremont or Ohio City run $250,000–$350,000; one-bedroom units rent for $900–$1,200.
- **Kansas City, Missouri**: Proximity to employers and affordable inventory make it a favorite for live-in landlords.
- **Memphis, Tennessee**: High yields, though you'll want to be selective on neighborhoods.
- **Oklahoma City, Oklahoma**: Fourplexes under $400,000 with per-unit rents near $850.

To illustrate: imagine you buy a $320,000 duplex in Indianapolis with 3.5% down ($11,200). Your FHA mortgage at 6.75% runs about $2,075/month (principal + interest), plus $220 for taxes and $100 for insurance—total $2,395. You rent the second unit for $1,350. Your net housing cost: $1,045 a month, and you're accumulating equity on a $320,000 asset instead of paying $1,600 for a one-bedroom apartment with no ownership stake.

In high-cost markets like San Francisco, Seattle, or Boston, the math flips. A $900,000 duplex might generate $3,200 in rent from the second unit, but your PITI could easily top $6,000. You're still subsidizing your housing, but you're not getting close to breakeven—and the higher carrying cost increases risk if a tenant moves out.

## The real costs you can't ignore

House hacking isn't free money; it's a business with expenses and obligations.

**Vacancy and turnover**: Rental units sit empty while you search for qualified tenants. Budget 5–10% of gross rents annually for vacancy losses. If your unit rents for $1,200, that's $60–$120/month in reserves.

**Maintenance and repairs**: You're now on the hook for two (or more) HVAC systems, water heaters, roofs, and appliances. Industry rule of thumb: set aside 1% of property value per year. On a $400,000 building, that's $4,000 annually—$333/month.

**Property management (if you outsource)**: Full-service managers charge 8–12% of collected rent. On $1,400/month, that's $112–$168. Many house hackers self-manage to save costs, but then *you're* fielding 10 p.m. calls about broken dishwashers.

**HOA fees and utilities**: If you're in a condo duplex or certain planned communities, HOA dues can run $150–$400/month. Check what utilities you're responsible for—some duplexes have shared meters, meaning you pay for your tenant's water or gas.

**Insurance riders**: Standard homeowner policies may not cover rental activities. You'll likely need a landlord or dwelling fire policy, which costs more.

**Tenant screening and legal compliance**: [NerdWallet recommends](https://www.nerdwallet.com/mortgages/learn/house-hacking-multifamily-property) working with your local Apartment Association to ensure background checks and screening criteria comply with fair housing laws. Use the same criteria for all applicants. Gross income should be 2.5–3× monthly rent; check credit, eviction history, and references.

## A worked example: Mia's duplex in Indianapolis

Let's return to Mia Chen. Here's her first-year P&L:

**Purchase price**: $385,000  
**Down payment (3.5%)**: $13,475  
**Closing costs**: ~$8,500  
**Total cash to close**: ~$22,000 (she saved aggressively and received a $5,000 gift from parents)

**Monthly expenses**:  
- Mortgage (P&I): $2,365  
- Property tax: $210  
- Insurance: $125  
- Maintenance reserve: $320  
- **Total**: $3,020

**Monthly rental income**: $1,400

**Net housing cost**: $1,620/month

Compared to her $2,100 Capitol Hill rent, Mia saves $480 monthly—$5,760 a year—while building equity in a $385,000 asset. She's also deducting mortgage interest, property tax, and a portion of maintenance on her Schedule E (consult a CPA for specifics; tax treatment of owner-occupied rentals can be complex).

After 12 months, she can move out, rent her original unit for another $1,400, and—assuming 10% vacancy and expenses—clear about $450/month in positive cash flow while repeating the house-hack strategy on a second property.

## The non-financial realities: privacy, proximity, and people

Money aside, house hacking is a lifestyle trade-off.

**You're always "home"**: There's no separation between residence and rental property. Lawn care, snow removal, tenant complaints—it's all on your doorstep. [Johnson Financial Group notes](https://www.johnsonfinancialgroup.com/resources/blogs/buying-or-building-a-home/what-is-house-hacking-the-real-estate-strategy-that-pays-toward-your-mortgage) you're "taking on landlord responsibilities, managing tenant relationships" while living next door.

**Tenant selection is existential**: A bad renter isn't just a financial headache; they're your neighbor. Late-night parties, unsupervised pets, passive-aggressive parking disputes—you can't escape. This makes rigorous screening non-negotiable. [Rocket Mortgage](https://www.rocketmortgage.com/learn/house-hacking) advises treating tenant placement with the gravity it deserves: credit check, income verification, prior landlord references, and a crystal-clear lease.

**Delayed gratification**: Your friends might be renting chic lofts downtown. You're mowing a duplex lawn in a working-class neighborhood and fielding maintenance texts on Saturday. The payoff is years away, in the form of accumulated equity and rental income that eventually funds your *next* move.

**Learning curve**: You're now a part-time property manager. Expect to learn local landlord-tenant law, fair housing regulations, lead paint disclosure rules (for pre-1978 buildings), security deposit limits, and eviction procedures. [Investopedia](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/mortgages-real-estate/09/so-you-wanna-be-a-landlord.asp) recommends connecting with your local Apartment Association and reading up on health and safety codes before you hand over keys.

## Where house hacking can go sideways

**Underestimating repair costs**: Old furnaces, leaky roofs, and corroded plumbing don't announce themselves during the home inspection. First-year surprises can blow through your emergency fund. Keep 3–6 months of total expenses liquid.

**Market timing risk**: If you buy at a local peak and rents soften, your cash flow evaporates. Unlike a passive index fund, real estate is illiquid—you can't sell half a duplex to rebalance.

**Overleveraging**: Some house hackers get greedy, stacking multiple FHA purchases or taking on properties they can't afford if a unit goes vacant. Remember: rental income is *potential* income. Underwrite to survive with zero rent for three months.

**Regulatory changes**: Zoning, rent control, eviction moratoria—local laws can shift. Cities like Seattle and Portland have added tenant protections that increase landlord costs and complexity.

**Personality mismatch**: If you're conflict-averse or value solitude, landlording—especially on-site—will drain you. Self-awareness matters.

## Who should (and shouldn't) house hack

**Good candidates**:
- First-time buyers priced out of single-family homes but able to save 3.5–10% for a multifamily down payment.
- Young professionals comfortable living modestly for 1–3 years to accelerate wealth-building.
- Anyone with basic handyman skills or the budget to hire contractors.
- People who see real estate as a long-term wealth engine, not a get-rich-quick scheme.

**Poor candidates**:
- Buyers who need pristine privacy or can't handle confrontation (evictions, lease enforcement).
- Those with unstable income—if you lose your job, the rental income helps but doesn't replace your W-2.
- Anyone in a market where duplex prices make the ROI laughable (think: Manhattan, Palo Alto, parts of coastal California).
- Individuals who lack an emergency fund—house hacking *requires* liquidity for repairs and vacancy.

## Tax and legal considerations

House hacking straddles the line between personal residence and rental property, creating a hybrid tax situation:

- **Mortgage interest deduction**: You can deduct interest on the portion of the property you occupy as your primary residence. The rental portion's interest goes on Schedule E.
- **Depreciation**: You may depreciate the rental portion of the building (not land) over 27.5 years. This is a non-cash deduction that can shelter rental income.
- **Repairs vs. improvements**: Day-to-day fixes (leaky faucet) are deductible; capital improvements (new roof) must be depreciated. The IRS draws bright lines; consult a CPA.
- **Security deposits**: In most states, these aren't income unless you keep them. They sit in escrow and are returned (minus legitimate deductions) at lease end.
- **1031 exchange limitations**: If you later sell and want to defer capital gains via a 1031 exchange, the portion you lived in complicates the calculation. Again, CPA territory.

**Fair housing law**: Federal, state, and local rules prohibit discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, or disability. Some cities add protections for source of income (e.g., Section 8 vouchers). [Investopedia notes](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/mortgages-real-estate/09/so-you-wanna-be-a-landlord.asp) that understanding Section 8 and tenant rights is essential for landlords in many markets.

**Landlord-tenant statutes**: Every state has rules governing security deposit limits, notice periods for entry, eviction procedures, and habitability standards. Violate these and you risk lawsuits, fines, or being unable to remove a non-paying tenant.

Do not wing this. Budget $500–$1,000 for an initial consult with a real estate attorney and a CPA who understands rental property taxation.

## Key takeaways

- House hacking lets first-time buyers purchase a two- to four-unit property, live in one unit, and rent the others to offset mortgage costs—sometimes eliminating housing expenses entirely.
- You can use FHA financing with as little as 3.5% down because the property is your primary residence, unlocking leverage unavailable to pure investors.
- The strategy works best in Midwest and Sun Belt markets where purchase prices are low relative to rents; coastal cities often don't pencil without significant cash reserves.
- Budget for vacancy (5–10% of rent), maintenance (1% of property value annually), insurance, and tenant turnover—house hacking is a business, not a passive income hack.
- Success requires rigorous tenant screening, fair housing compliance, and emotional stamina for on-site landlord duties; if you value privacy or hate confrontation, reconsider.
