# Dollar-Cost Averaging When Markets Feel Scary: Why Autopilot Beats Market Timing

When the S&P 500 dropped 19.4% in 2022, panic selling cost ordinary investors billions. When COVID sent markets into freefall in March 2020, the same thing happened. And when the next correction arrives—[3 in 4 Americans now expect one](https://money.com/stock-market-record-highs-investing-strategy)—the pattern will likely repeat unless investors have a concrete plan.

The hardest part about investing isn't finding good funds. It's not panicking when your account balance turns red.

[One couple recently pulled $600,000 from stocks](https://www.benzinga.com/personal-finance/management/26/10/62233556/this-couple-pulled-600000-from-the-stock-market-when-things-got-scary-now-theyre-fighting-over-who-made-them-miss-the-rebound) near a market bottom, then watched helplessly as the rebound added hundreds of thousands to portfolios they'd abandoned. Their mistake wasn't bad timing on the way out—it was never having a system for the way back in.

That system exists. It's called dollar-cost averaging, and it's designed specifically for moments when markets feel terrifying.

## What dollar-cost averaging actually means

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing a fixed dollar amount at regular intervals—weekly, monthly, quarterly—regardless of what the market is doing. You don't try to predict peaks or bottoms. You just invest $500 every month, or $250 every paycheck, whether the S&P 500 is up 10% or down 15%.

[According to the Financial Industry Regulatory Authority](https://blog.massmutual.com/retiring-investing/dollar-cost-averaging), "By setting up a disciplined schedule of investments that you make regardless of market fluctuations, dollar-cost averaging can remove some of the emotion from investing and might help you avoid making impulsive decisions."

The mechanics are simple. Let's say you commit to investing $600 monthly in a total market index fund:

- **Month 1**: Market price $100/share → you buy 6 shares
- **Month 2**: Price drops to $75/share → you buy 8 shares
- **Month 3**: Price rises to $90/share → you buy 6.67 shares

After three months you own 20.67 shares at an average cost of $87.07 per share—better than the $100 you'd have paid if you'd invested all $1,800 on day one, and significantly better than sitting in cash waiting for the "perfect" moment that never arrives.

## Why your brain hates buying when stocks are falling

Here's the psychological trap: when markets drop 10%, every instinct screams to wait. "What if it drops another 10%?" When it does drop further, the voice gets louder. "See? Good thing you waited. What if it drops *another* 10%?"

By the time the coast feels clear, you've missed the entire recovery.

[NerdWallet points out](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/dollar-cost-averaging-2) that DCA "can save investors from their psychological biases. Because investors swing between fear and greed, they are prone to making emotional trading decisions as the market gyrates."

Meet Priya, 34, who works in healthcare IT and earns $78,000 annually. In March 2020 she had $35,000 saved for investing—money she'd kept in a high-yield savings account while "waiting for the right time" to get into the market. When COVID crashed markets by 34%, she froze. "What if I invest now and it drops another 20%?" she thought.

Instead of investing the lump sum, Priya split it into 12 chunks of roughly $2,900. She invested one chunk on the 15th of every month from April 2020 through March 2021, regardless of headlines.

- Some months she bought when markets had recovered 10%
- Other months she bought during summer pullbacks
- She never bought at the absolute bottom (impossible to predict)
- She also never bought at the peak (equally impossible to predict)

By March 2025, that $35,000 had grown to approximately $68,000—a 94% gain, compared to the $56,000 she'd have if she'd kept waiting in cash earning 1.5% annually.

The math worked because Priya removed emotion from the equation. She didn't need to be smart or brave. She just needed to be systematic.

## The lump sum vs. dollar-cost averaging debate

Financial economists will correctly note that historically, investing a lump sum immediately outperforms dollar-cost averaging about 66% of the time. Markets trend upward over long periods, so delaying investment means missing growth.

But here's what academic papers miss: **most people aren't deciding between lump sum and DCA—they're deciding between DCA and doing nothing**.

When you inherit $50,000 or get a bonus or sell a house, the theoretical optimal move is to invest it all immediately in a diversified portfolio. In practice, most people can't psychologically pull that trigger, especially during volatile periods. The fear of buying right before a crash is paralyzing.

[Charles Schwab research shows](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/what-is-dollar-cost-averaging) that DCA "may also help prevent your emotions from undermining your portfolio. When you invest a large sum of money in a single trade, you're more likely to feel regret if that trade turns out to be poorly timed."

Dollar-cost averaging accepts a small mathematical disadvantage in exchange for a massive behavioral advantage. You're more likely to actually follow through. And a plan you execute beats a perfect plan you abandon.

## How to set up dollar-cost averaging in practice

**For retirement accounts**: If you're contributing to a 401(k) or IRA through paycheck deductions, congratulations—you're already dollar-cost averaging. [Money magazine notes](https://money.com/stock-market-record-highs-investing-strategy) that continuing those contributions during market downturns is "your best chance to buy equities at a discount."

Don't pause contributions when markets fall. That's exactly when DCA delivers the most value.

**For lump sums**: If you have $24,000 sitting in savings that you want to invest:

1. Divide it into 12 equal portions ($2,000 each)
2. Set up automatic transfers from savings to your brokerage on the same day each month
3. Set up automatic purchases of your target fund(s) when the cash hits your account
4. Most brokerages (Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, etc.) allow scheduled recurring investments

**Time frame**: Spreading a lump sum over 6–12 months is common. Longer than 18 months and you're likely overthinking it. Shorter than three months and you're essentially making a lump-sum investment with extra steps.

**What to buy**: DCA works best with broad, diversified index funds—total stock market, total international, S&P 500. The strategy makes less sense for individual stocks, which can go to zero regardless of how you average in.

## The couple who pulled $600,000 and what they should do now

[According to reporting from Benzinga](https://www.benzinga.com/personal-finance/management/26/10/62233556/this-couple-pulled-600000-from-the-stock-market-when-things-got-scary-now-theyre-fighting-over-who-made-them-miss-the-rebound), the couple sitting on $600,000 in cash faces a common predicament: they know they need to get back into markets, but the fear of buying right before another drop is overwhelming.

Here's a realistic path forward:

**Step 1**: Determine true emergency reserves. The reporting notes that "a common approach is to keep several months of expenses in an insured, accessible account as a buffer, so a future downturn doesn't force them to sell investments to cover a surprise bill." If their monthly expenses are $8,000, they should keep $48,000–$72,000 (six to nine months) in a high-yield savings account earning 4–5%. That's their safety net.

**Step 2**: Invest the remaining ~$540,000 over 12–18 months. At 12 months, that's $45,000 per month. At 18 months, it's $30,000 per month. Choose the time frame that lets them sleep at night.

**Step 3**: Automate everything. Set calendar reminders, but better yet, schedule the transfers through their bank and brokerage. Manual execution invites second-guessing.

**Step 4**: Build an actual investment policy. What asset allocation (stocks vs. bonds)? Which specific funds? Under what circumstances would they sell? Having this written down before the next crisis prevents emotional decisions.

The mistake wasn't selling during the downturn—fear is human. The mistake was having no plan for getting back in, which turned a temporary panic into a permanent opportunity cost.

## When dollar-cost averaging doesn't help

DCA isn't a magic shield. It doesn't protect you from:

- **Bear markets that stay down for years**: If you DCA into Japan's market starting in 1990, you'd have waited decades for recovery. Geographic diversification (U.S. + international) matters.
- **Bad investments**: Averaging into a failing company or a speculative bubble just means you lose money more slowly.
- **Tax-advantaged account limits**: If you have $7,000 to invest in a Roth IRA and the annual limit is $7,000, waiting to DCA means missing months of tax-free growth you can never recapture. Just invest the full amount.
- **Persistent avoidance**: Some people use DCA as an excuse to never be fully invested. "I'll invest more when the market drops another 5%... okay, another 5%..." Decades pass. Don't let perfect be the enemy of participating.

[The SEC warns](https://www.sec.gov/investor/pubs/tenthingstoconsider.htm) that while dollar-cost averaging "can protect yourself from the risk of investing all of your money at the wrong time," it doesn't eliminate risk—you can still lose money if your investments decline in value.

## The real advantage is staying invested through the scary parts

Markets will fall again. That's not pessimism—it's math. Since 1928, the S&P 500 has experienced:

- A 10% correction every ~12 months on average
- A 20% bear market every 3–4 years
- Multiple 50%+ crashes per century

[Fidelity research on bear market investing](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/personal-finance/bear-market-investing) emphasizes that "for long-term investors, a market downturn can simply mean stocks and other investments are on sale."

The investors who build wealth aren't the ones who perfectly time these events. They're the ones who keep buying through them.

Dollar-cost averaging is training wheels for that discipline. You commit to buying $X every month regardless of headlines, and over time you internalize that volatility is normal—even advantageous. You're buying more shares when prices are depressed.

Eventually, you stop checking your balance during corrections because you know the automatic investments are working. You stop watching financial news every evening because it doesn't change your plan. The strategy becomes boring, which is exactly the point.

## Building the habit before you need it

If you're reading this during calm markets, set up DCA now:

- Increase your 401(k) contribution by 1–2%
- Start a $100/month automatic investment into a Roth IRA
- If you have a lump sum, begin the systematic investing plan today

If you're reading this during a market crash, congratulations—you're getting the discount pricing that makes DCA most effective. Your future self will thank you for the shares you're accumulating while everyone else is hiding in cash.

If you're reading this while sitting on cash you pulled from markets, forgive yourself for the past decision and implement a DCA plan for the next 6–12 months. You can't undo missing the rebound, but you can avoid missing the next decade of growth.

The hard truth: there will never be a moment when investing feels completely safe. The market could always drop tomorrow. Dollar-cost averaging doesn't eliminate that uncertainty—it gives you a way to move forward despite it.

For tax considerations specific to your lump-sum scenario, or for help building an investment policy statement tailored to your goals, consult a fee-only CFP. For questions about capital gains from selling old positions, talk to a CPA. 

What you can do today, right now, is open your brokerage app and set up that first automatic investment.

## Key takeaways

- Dollar-cost averaging means investing fixed amounts at regular intervals regardless of market conditions, removing the psychological burden of timing the market perfectly.

- Historically lump-sum investing outperforms DCA about 66% of the time, but DCA dramatically increases the likelihood you'll actually follow through rather than freeze in fear.

- For lump sums like windfalls or cash positions, spreading investments over 6–18 months balances mathematical efficiency with psychological comfort.

- If you contribute to a 401(k) or IRA through paycheck deductions, you're already dollar-cost averaging—the key is never pausing during downturns.

- DCA is a training tool for the real skill: staying invested through bear markets, corrections, and scary headlines without panic-selling.
