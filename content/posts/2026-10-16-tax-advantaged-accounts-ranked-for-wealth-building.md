# Tax-Advantaged Accounts Ranked: The Ladder Every Wealth Builder Should Climb

The conventional wisdom says "just max out your 401(k)" — but that's like telling someone to save money without explaining *which* dollar delivers the biggest punch.

Not all tax-advantaged accounts are created equal. Some offer a single tax break; others triple your advantage. Some lock your money until you're 59½; others let you access it penalty-free under the right conditions. And if you're climbing the income ladder — or already at the top — the order in which you fill these buckets can mean tens (or hundreds) of thousands of extra dollars over a career.

More than 32,000 Americans now hold $10 million or more in tax-advantaged accounts, with over 1,000 holding *mega-IRAs* in the nine figures, according to [Kiplinger's reporting on strategic trusts](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/building-mega-ira-only-half-100000260.html). But you don't need a Thiel-level self-directed IRA to benefit from smart sequencing. The ranked ladder below applies whether you're earning $60,000 or $600,000 — though the *amounts* you can stuff into each tier will vary.

We'll rank the six major account types by *effective tax advantage per dollar*, factoring in contribution limits, flexibility, and inheritance rules. Then we'll show you exactly how much a disciplined 35-year-old can accumulate by climbing this ladder in order.

## The ranking: From nuclear tax advantage to "still pretty good"

### 1. HSA (Health Savings Account) — the triple-threat champion

**Max contribution (2025):** $4,300 individual / $8,550 family (+$1,000 catch-up at 55+)  
**Tax advantage:** Pre-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses. No other account offers three tax breaks.

If you have a high-deductible health plan (HDHP) — defined in 2025 as a deductible of at least $1,650 individual or $3,300 family — the HSA is your [most powerful wealth-building tool in the tax code](https://www.marketwatch.com/picks/this-is-one-of-the-most-powerful-wealth-building-tools-in-the-tax-code-says-the-founder-of-ira-financial-1ce6b153), per Adam Bergman, founder of IRA Financial.

Unlike flexible spending accounts (FSAs), HSA balances roll over indefinitely. Unlike Roth IRAs, contributions are *deductible*. And unlike 401(k)s, withdrawals for medical expenses are tax-free at any age.

The sleeper move: invest your HSA balance instead of spending it. Pay current medical bills out-of-pocket, keep the receipts, and let your HSA compound for decades. [Fidelity's analysis of the triple tax advantage](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/wealth-management-insights/hsa-triple-tax-advantages) shows how pre-tax contributions grow tax-deferred, then come out tax-free — a trifecta no other account matches.

After age 65, you can withdraw HSA funds for *any* reason — you'll owe ordinary income tax (just like a traditional IRA), but no penalty. In effect, an HSA becomes a traditional IRA with a bonus medical-expense escape hatch.

**Case study snapshot:** Lena, 35, contributes $4,300/year to her HSA and invests it in a low-cost index fund. She pays her $2,000 annual medical bills from checking and saves the receipts. At 7% growth, by age 65 she'll have roughly $435,000 — all accessible tax-free if she reimburses herself for those saved receipts (or for any post-retirement medical costs). If she'd put that same $4,300 into a taxable brokerage at a 24% marginal rate, she'd net about $310,000 after capital gains. The HSA wins by $125,000.

### 2. Roth 401(k) / Roth 403(b) (with employer match) — free money meets tax-free growth

**Max contribution (2025):** $23,500 employee deferral (+$7,500 catch-up at 50+)  
**Tax advantage:** After-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals in retirement. Employer match goes into a traditional (pre-tax) account, but the match itself is still free money.

Why Roth over traditional here? Two reasons:

1. **Tax diversification.** You'll likely have *some* traditional/pre-tax money (including that employer match). Adding Roth contributions gives you tax-free buckets to draw from in retirement, letting you manage your taxable income.
2. **No RMDs on Roth 401(k) balances** (as of SECURE 2.0). You can let Roth 401(k) money sit and grow indefinitely, or roll it to a Roth IRA for even cleaner estate planning.

If your employer offers a match — say, 50% of the first 6% you contribute — *always* contribute at least enough to capture the full match. That's an instant 50% (or 100%, depending on the formula) return before any market gains.

**Example:** Jayden, 28, earns $85,000. His company matches 50% of the first 6% he defers. He contributes $5,100 (6% of salary) to the Roth 401(k); his employer adds $2,550 to a traditional 401(k). That $2,550 is a risk-free 50% gain. If he compounds that combined $7,650/year at 7% until age 65, he'll have about $1.26 million — roughly $850,000 in the Roth (tax-free) and $410,000 in the traditional (taxable on withdrawal). Without the match, his $5,100 alone would grow to only $840,000.

### 3. Roth IRA — flexibility meets tax-free compounding

**Max contribution (2025):** $7,000 (+$1,000 catch-up at 50+)  
**Income phase-out (2025):** Begins at $150,000 (single) / $236,000 (married filing jointly)  
**Tax advantage:** After-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified withdrawals. Contributions (but not earnings) can be withdrawn anytime, penalty-free.

The Roth IRA is the Swiss Army knife of tax-advantaged accounts. You can pull out your *contributions* at any age without tax or penalty (though you shouldn't unless it's an emergency). Earnings can be withdrawn tax- and penalty-free after age 59½, provided the account has been open at least five years.

High earners often use the *backdoor Roth* strategy: contribute $7,000 to a non-deductible traditional IRA, then immediately convert it to a Roth. (Consult a CPA if you have existing pre-tax IRA balances — the pro-rata rule can complicate this.)

Why rank the Roth IRA below the Roth 401(k)? **Contribution limits.** You can shelter $23,500 in a Roth 401(k) vs. just $7,000 in a Roth IRA. But for anyone who's already maxing the 401(k) match, the Roth IRA is the next priority — it's more flexible than a traditional IRA and offers better estate-planning benefits (beneficiaries inherit tax-free growth).

### 4. Traditional 401(k) / 403(b) (beyond the match) — upfront deduction, deferred tax

**Max contribution (2025):** $23,500 employee deferral (+$7,500 catch-up)  
**Tax advantage:** Pre-tax contributions reduce current taxable income; growth is tax-deferred; withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income.

Once you've captured the employer match (via Roth or traditional deferrals) and maxed your Roth IRA, additional 401(k) contributions — whether Roth or traditional — are your next lever.

Choose *traditional* 401(k) contributions if:

- You're in a high tax bracket now (32%+) and expect to be in a lower bracket in retirement.
- You want to lower your current adjusted gross income (AGI) to qualify for other credits or deductions.

Choose *Roth* 401(k) if:

- You're early in your career and your tax rate is likely to rise.
- You value tax diversification and want more tax-free income in retirement.

**Solo 401(k) for the self-employed:** If you're a freelancer or side-hustler, a solo 401(k) (also called an individual 401(k)) lets you contribute as both employee ($23,500 max) *and* employer (up to 25% of compensation, combined limit $69,000 in 2025). [IRA Financial's founder calls this "one of the most powerful wealth-building tools"](https://www.marketwatch.com/picks/this-is-one-of-the-most-powerful-wealth-building-tools-in-the-tax-code-says-the-founder-of-ira-financial-1ce6b153) for high earners with self-employment income. It also unlocks the *mega backdoor Roth*: if your plan allows after-tax contributions beyond the $23,500 limit, you can contribute (and immediately convert) an extra ~$45,500 to Roth.

### 5. Traditional IRA — the fallback deduction (if you qualify)

**Max contribution (2025):** $7,000 (+$1,000 catch-up)  
**Deduction phase-out (2025):** If you (or your spouse) have a workplace retirement plan, the deduction begins phasing out at $79,000 (single) / $126,000 (married filing jointly)  
**Tax advantage:** Potentially deductible contributions, tax-deferred growth, taxable withdrawals.

For most people, the traditional IRA is a lower priority than the Roth IRA — especially if you can't deduct the contribution due to income limits. A non-deductible traditional IRA (contributions made with after-tax money, but earnings still taxed on withdrawal) is generally worse than a taxable brokerage account, because you lose favorable long-term capital-gains treatment and pay ordinary income tax on all gains.

*However,* if you're under the income threshold and can take the full deduction, a traditional IRA can make sense if you're in a high bracket now and expect a lower one in retirement.

The traditional IRA is also the gateway for backdoor Roth conversions (see #3).

### 6. Taxable brokerage account — liquidity with long-term capital-gains treatment

**Max contribution:** None  
**Tax advantage:** Long-term capital gains (assets held >1 year) taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on income; qualified dividends also get preferential rates.

Once you've maxed all the tax-advantaged space you can access, every additional dollar goes here. [Wealth advisors surveyed by MoneyLion](https://www.moneylion.com/trending/money/wealth-advisors-how-high-earners-put-idle-cash-to-work) recommend high earners prioritize tax-advantaged accounts before taxable brokerage, but the brokerage account has one superpower: **liquidity**. No age restrictions, no early-withdrawal penalties, no required minimum distributions.

Taxable accounts also benefit from the *step-up in basis* at death. If you buy stock at $50/share and it's worth $200/share when you die, your heirs inherit it at $200 — erasing all capital gains. (Roth accounts inherit tax-free anyway, so the step-up doesn't matter there; traditional IRAs and 401(k)s do *not* get a step-up and are fully taxable to heirs.)

For investors in the 0% long-term capital-gains bracket (roughly <$48,350 taxable income for singles, <$96,700 for married couples in 2025), selling appreciated stock in a taxable account can be completely tax-free — a feature no traditional IRA can match.

**Pro tip:** Use taxable accounts for tax-loss harvesting. If you sell a losing position to offset gains, you can immediately buy a similar (but not "substantially identical") fund to stay invested. You can't do this in an IRA or 401(k) because there's no capital-gains tax to offset.

## Honorable mentions: 529 plans, donor-advised funds, and CRTs

- **529 education savings plans:** Contributions aren't federally deductible (though many states offer a deduction), but growth and withdrawals for qualified education expenses are tax-free. If your state offers a deduction, prioritize a 529 before taxable brokerage — but after retirement accounts (you can borrow for college; you can't borrow for retirement).
  
- **Donor-advised funds (DAFs):** Immediate tax deduction for contributions; tax-free growth; grants to charity over time. Great for bunching charitable deductions in high-income years.

- **Charitable remainder trusts (CRTs) and other irrevocable trusts:** Used by ultra-high-net-worth individuals to defer or eliminate capital-gains taxes while generating income. [Kiplinger's mega-IRA piece](https://finance.yahoo.com/economy/policy/articles/building-mega-ira-only-half-100000260.html) notes that building a huge tax-deferred account is only half the battle — without strategic trusts, heirs can face crippling ordinary income tax on inherited IRAs under the SECURE Act's 10-year rule.

## The 30-year ladder climb: A worked example

Meet **Priya, 35**, a software engineer earning $120,000. She's single, lives in a state with no income tax, and has an HDHP. Her employer offers a 401(k) with a 4% match (dollar-for-dollar on the first 4% she defers). Here's her annual savings sequence:

1. **HSA:** Contributes $4,300 (max), invests it all. Tax savings at 24% marginal rate: **$1,032**. Effective cost: $3,268.
2. **Roth 401(k):** Contributes 4% ($4,800) to capture the full $4,800 match. Total going in: $9,600. After-tax cost to her: $4,800.
3. **Roth IRA:** Contributes $7,000 (max). After-tax cost: $7,000.
4. **Roth 401(k) (additional):** Adds another $13,900 to hit the $23,500 employee max. After-tax cost: $13,900.
5. **Taxable brokerage:** Contributes remaining $5,000.

**Total saved:** $44,200/year (about 37% of gross income).  
**Out-of-pocket after HSA deduction:** ~$43,200.

Assuming 7% average annual return over 30 years:

- **HSA (tax-free):** ~$435,000
- **Roth 401(k) (tax-free):** ~$2.18 million ($870k from her $18,700/year + $1.31M from the $4,800 match + growth)
- **Roth IRA (tax-free):** ~$708,000
- **Taxable brokerage (long-term cap gains):** ~$505,000 (assume 15% LTCG rate on ~$355k in gains = ~$53k tax, net ~$452k after-tax)

**Grand total at age 65:** Roughly **$3.78 million**, with $3.32 million completely tax-free and ~$452k in after-tax brokerage.

If Priya had instead put everything into a taxable brokerage (no tax-advantaged accounts), her $44,200/year would grow to about $4.47M — but she'd owe 15% LTCG on ~$3.14M in gains (~$471k tax), netting ~$4.0M. She'd also lose the employer match entirely (no 401(k) = no match), dropping her to ~$3.55M after tax. **By using the ladder, she's ~$770k richer** (and that's before considering RMDs, estate taxes, and loss of flexibility).

## Common mistakes (and how to avoid them)

1. **Skipping the HSA because "I'm healthy."** Health costs are unavoidable over a lifetime. Even if you never get sick, you can withdraw HSA funds penalty-free after 65 for any purpose (taxed like a traditional IRA). You're leaving free tax arbitrage on the table.

2. **Choosing traditional 401(k) in your 20s.** Unless you're already in the 32%+ bracket, Roth contributions are almost always better early in your career. Your tax rate will likely be higher later.

3. **Contributing to a Roth IRA while not maxing the 401(k) match.** The match is a guaranteed return. Capture it first, even if it means contributing to a traditional 401(k) instead of Roth.

4. **Ignoring the mega backdoor Roth.** If you're self-employed or your employer plan allows after-tax contributions and in-service conversions, you can funnel an extra $40k+/year into Roth. Check your plan document or ask HR.

5. **Letting FOMO drive brokerage speculation before filling tax-advantaged buckets.** Meme stocks and crypto feel exciting, but compounding $23,500/year in a Roth 401(k) from age 25 to 65 at 7% yields $4.7 million tax-free. A taxable account needs to beat that after paying 15–20% on gains. Very few day-traders do.

## When to get help

If your income is variable (commissions, bonuses, stock comp), if you're facing a liquidity event (startup exit, inheritance), or if you have multiple income streams (W-2 + side business + rental property), consult a **fee-only CFP® or CPA**. The sequencing above is a strong default, but:

- High earners in the 35–37% bracket may benefit from maxing traditional 401(k) first to lower current AGI, then converting to Roth in lower-income years.
- If you expect to leave a large estate, Roth conversions during low-income years can reduce future RMDs and estate-tax exposure.
- Complex situations (non-deductible IRA balances, employer stock in a 401(k), state tax considerations) require personalized modeling.

[NerdWallet's guide to tax-efficient investing](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/tax-efficient-investing) recommends consulting an advisor "if tackling investing while weighing tax consequences makes your head spin" — and there's no shame in that. A one-time financial plan (often $1,500–$3,000) can pay for itself many times over.

## Key takeaways

1. **The HSA is the single best tax-advantaged account** — triple tax benefit, no RMDs, and penalty-free access after 65. If you're eligible, max it first.
2. **Always capture the full employer 401(k) match** before contributing elsewhere. A 50% or 100% instant return beats any tax arbitrage.
3. **Roth accounts (401(k) and IRA) are rocket fuel for long-term wealth** — decades of tax-free compounding and no RMDs mean more control in retirement and cleaner inheritance for heirs.
4. **Traditional accounts still have a place** — if you're in a high bracket now (32%+) and expect lower income in retirement, traditional 401(k) contributions can cut your current tax bill and let you convert to Roth in lean years.
5. **Taxable brokerage is the overflow tank, not the foundation** — max tax-advantaged space first, but don't fear taxable accounts. They offer liquidity, step-up in basis, and tax-loss harvesting opportunities that retirement accounts can't match.
