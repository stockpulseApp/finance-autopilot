# Dollar-Cost Averaging When Markets Feel Scary: The Numbers Behind Staying the Course

The stock market doesn't care about your feelings. That's a problem, because your feelings about the stock market can cost you serious money.

When headlines scream about corrections, recessions, or geopolitical chaos, the instinct to freeze—or worse, to sell—kicks in hard. A recent survey by MarketWise found that retail investors who made emotional trading decisions lost an average of $2,237 per trade. That's not a rounding error. That's a vacation, a few mortgage payments, or three months of groceries vaporized because fear hijacked logic.

The irony? The strategy that works best during scary markets is also the simplest: keep buying. Specifically, keep buying the same dollar amount on the same schedule, regardless of whether the Dow is soaring or cratering. This approach—dollar-cost averaging—isn't sexy, and it won't make you feel clever at cocktail parties. But it does something far more valuable: it removes the emotional land mines from investing and lets math do the heavy lifting.

## What dollar-cost averaging actually is (and isn't)

Dollar-cost averaging (DCA) means investing a fixed amount of money at regular intervals—say, $300 every two weeks or $500 on the first of each month—into the same investment, no matter what the market is doing. [According to Merrill Lynch, this strategy involves investing fixed amounts regularly, which helps reduce the impact of price fluctuations](https://eciks.org/20363-66423-invest-2026-index-funds-iras-strategy).

Here's how it works in practice. Imagine you invest $500 monthly into a low-cost S&P 500 index fund. In January, the share price is $100, so you buy five shares. In February, the market dips and shares cost $80. Your $500 now buys 6.25 shares. In March, prices rebound to $110, and you buy 4.55 shares. Over three months, you've invested $1,500 and accumulated 15.8 shares at an average cost of roughly $94.94 per share—better than two of the three months' prices.

The key insight: when prices drop, your fixed investment buys *more* shares. When prices rise, it buys fewer. Over time, this tends to lower your average cost per share compared to buying randomly or—worse—only when you "feel good" about the market.

[As FINRA notes, with dollar-cost averaging, you invest your money in equal portions at regular intervals, allowing you to buy more shares when prices are low and fewer when they're high](https://eciks.org/20363-66423-invest-2026-index-funds-iras-strategy).

What DCA is *not*: a tool for picking which stocks or funds to buy. It won't tell you whether to invest in tech, bonds, or real estate. It's purely a method for *when* and *how much* to deploy capital you've already decided to invest. [Fidelity explains that dollar-cost averaging is not a strategy for deciding what to invest in—rather, it can help you take the stress out of deciding when to invest](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/personal-finance/bear-market-investing).

## Why your brain hates buying when markets tank

Behavioral finance has a name for the psychological trap that snares most investors: loss aversion. Research shows people feel the pain of a loss roughly twice as intensely as the pleasure of an equivalent gain. So watching your portfolio drop 10% feels far worse than watching it rise 10% feels good.

This wiring made sense when our ancestors faced existential threats—better to avoid the tiger than chase the gazelle. But in investing, loss aversion leads to terrible decisions. [A survey found that feelings of anxiety about stock market swings override even the soundest financial plan, causing investors to panic-sell at precisely the wrong moment](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/feeling-anxious-stock-market-most-163000254.html).

[Charles Schwab points out that when you invest a large sum of money in a single trade, you're more likely to feel regret if that trade turns out to be poorly timed. But with dollar-cost averaging, you're investing smaller sums of money over time, making each individual purchase feel less consequential](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/what-is-dollar-cost-averaging).

Enter DCA as psychological armor. By committing to invest a fixed amount on a fixed schedule, you short-circuit the emotional roller coaster. You don't have to decide whether "now" is a good time. You don't have to interpret the latest inflation report or Fed minutes. You just execute the plan. [RBC Global Asset Management notes that investing a large amount all at once can be intimidating, and fear of volatility can lead investors to delay or avoid making the investment altogether](https://www.rbcgam.com/en/ca/learn-plan/investment-basics/understanding-dollar-cost-averaging-vs-lump-sum-investing/detail).

## The math: DCA vs. lump-sum investing

Here's where it gets interesting. Academics and financial institutions have run the numbers on DCA versus lump-sum investing (investing all your available cash at once), and the results are surprisingly consistent: lump-sum investing usually wins.

Why? Because markets trend upward over time. If you have $12,000 sitting in cash and you invest it all on January 1, you're immediately exposed to a full year of potential gains. If you spread that $12,000 over twelve monthly $1,000 investments, the later dollars miss out on months of growth. [Thrivent notes that historically, lump-sum investing has often led to higher returns](https://www.thrivent.com/insights/investing/pros-cons-of-dollar-cost-averaging-vs-lump-sum-investing).

But—and this is a crucial but—that analysis assumes you *have* a lump sum and are psychologically capable of deploying it when markets are volatile or downright scary. Most people don't have a pile of cash waiting to invest. They have paychecks. And even those with lump sums often freeze when headlines turn dark.

[Kiplinger observes that trying to predict the market's short-term direction is extraordinarily difficult, and emotional pressure during turbulent periods works directly against long-term returns](https://www.kiplinger.com/investing/there-is-no-perfect-moment-to-invest). In that context, DCA isn't about maximizing theoretical returns; it's about maximizing the returns you'll *actually achieve* given human psychology.

Consider this: if DCA keeps you invested during a bear market when you'd otherwise flee to cash, it's a massive win. [American Century research continues to show that staying consistently invested in the market has the potential to outperform market timing over the long term](https://www.americancentury.com/insights/dollar-cost-averaging).

## A worked example: Emily's 2022 bear market

Let's make this concrete. Emily, a 34-year-old marketing manager earning $78,000 annually, started investing in January 2022 with $500 per month into a Vanguard S&P 500 ETF (VOO). Terrible timing, right? The S&P 500 peaked in early January 2022 and proceeded to fall nearly 25% by October.

Here's what happened to Emily's investments over 12 months:

- **January–March 2022:** VOO averages around $410/share. Emily buys roughly 3.66 shares ($1,500 ÷ $410).
- **April–June 2022:** Market slides; VOO averages $380. She buys 3.95 shares.
- **July–September 2022:** Volatility intensifies; VOO averages $360. She buys 4.17 shares.
- **October–December 2022:** Market bottoms then recovers slightly; VOO averages $355. She buys 4.23 shares.

Total invested: $6,000. Total shares: roughly 16.01. Average cost per share: about $374.77.

By December 2022, VOO was trading around $365—below her average cost. Her portfolio showed a small paper loss. But Emily kept the plan. By December 2023, VOO had rebounded to roughly $440. Her 16.01 shares were worth about $7,044—a 17.4% gain on her original $6,000, plus she'd added another $6,000 in 2023, buying shares at an average lower cost during the recovery.

Had Emily tried to "wait for the right moment" in 2022, she likely would have sat in cash all year, missed buying shares at $355–$360, and entered 2023 with zero invested. DCA forced her to buy during the pain—and that's when the best deals appear.

## When DCA shines (and when it doesn't)

Dollar-cost averaging works best in a few specific situations:

**You're investing from regular income.** If you're putting part of each paycheck into a 401(k) or IRA, you're already doing DCA. This is the default mode for most retirement savers, and it's effective precisely because it's automatic.

**You're paralyzed by market volatility.** If checking your portfolio makes your stomach churn and you're tempted to wait for "calmer" times, DCA gives you permission to ignore the noise. [Yahoo Finance notes that many of the S&P 500's strongest trading days occur during bear markets when volatility is high, and sitting in cash until after the recovery has begun usually hampers long-term returns](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/history-reveals-buying-vanguard-p-120500454.html).

**You have a long time horizon.** DCA is a multi-year strategy. [If you'll need your money within a couple of years, the odds may not be good enough to take the chance that you'll avoid a bear market](https://finance.yahoo.com/markets/stocks/articles/history-reveals-buying-vanguard-p-120500454.html). For money you need soon, DCA into stocks is the wrong tool.

**You lack a large lump sum.** Most people don't inherit $50,000 or sell a business. They save monthly. DCA is the natural fit.

When does DCA *not* make sense? If you're sitting on a windfall—inheritance, bonus, home sale proceeds—and you have a long time horizon, the data suggests lump-sum investing typically delivers better results. But only if you can stomach the regret if the market drops 15% the month after you invest. [Investopedia quotes financial advisors saying that even if the market crashes the day you invest, time in the market matters more than timing the market](https://www.investopedia.com/overcome-your-investing-fears-11756674).

There's no shame in choosing DCA for psychological comfort, even if it sacrifices a few percentage points of theoretical return. A plan you'll stick with beats a theoretically optimal plan you'll abandon.

## DCA in practice: automate everything

The power of dollar-cost averaging evaporates if you have to manually execute trades every month. Life gets busy. Markets get scary. You'll skip a month, then two, then quit.

Automation solves this. Set up automatic transfers from your checking account to your brokerage or retirement account on the same day each month. Most brokerages—Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, etc.—allow you to auto-invest those transfers into specific funds.

If you're investing in a 401(k), you're already automated via payroll deductions. If you're using an IRA or taxable brokerage account, spend 10 minutes setting up recurring transfers and purchases. Then forget about it.

One pro tip: align your investment date with your paycheck. If you're paid on the 15th and 30th, set your investment for the 16th or the 1st. The money moves before you can spend it on anything else, and you avoid the cognitive load of "finding" money to invest each month.

## The limits of DCA: it won't save you from bad investments

Dollar-cost averaging is a deployment strategy, not a substitute for due diligence. If you're DCA'ing into a speculative stock, a poorly diversified fund, or a crypto project with no fundamentals, you're just buying garbage in installments.

DCA works best with broad, low-cost index funds—S&P 500, total U.S. stock market, or global stock funds—that give you exposure to hundreds or thousands of companies. [KB Asset Management recently announced that their US benchmark index ETFs, which offer dollar-cost-averaged exposure to core US indices like the S&P 500 and Nasdaq 100, have surpassed $2.1 billion in net assets](https://finance.biggo.com/news/4af3d07e-15bb-4ac7-be18-949c6845aeb6), highlighting growing investor confidence in this approach.

Also, DCA doesn't eliminate risk. Markets can fall for years. If you started DCA in 2000 into the S&P 500, you endured a brutal decade. But even then, the investors who kept buying through 2002 and 2008–2009 ended up with enviable portfolios by 2010 and beyond.

## Tax considerations and when to consult a professional

Dollar-cost averaging in tax-advantaged accounts (401(k), IRA, Roth IRA) is straightforward—you don't pay taxes on gains until withdrawal (traditional) or not at all (Roth). But in taxable brokerage accounts, every sale is a taxable event.

If you're DCA'ing into a taxable account, be mindful of wash-sale rules if you're trading frequently, and consider tax-loss harvesting if you're sitting on losses. For most people DCA'ing into index funds, this isn't a daily concern, but if your portfolio grows large or your situation is complex—high income, multiple accounts, inheritance—talk to a CPA or fee-only CFP. They can help you optimize contributions, Roth conversions, and withdrawal strategies.

## The real risk: doing nothing

The biggest danger isn't buying at the "wrong" time. It's never buying at all.

Cash sitting in a checking account earns minimal interest and loses purchasing power to inflation. Waiting for the "perfect" moment to invest usually means waiting forever, because perfect moments only reveal themselves in hindsight.

[NerdWallet notes that dollar-cost averaging can save investors from their psychological biases, because committing to this strategy means you'll be investing when the market is down—and that's when investors can potentially score the best deals](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/dollar-cost-averaging-2).

Markets reward patience and consistency. They punish hesitation and trying to be clever. DCA gives you a framework to stay in the game when your instincts scream to run.

## Key takeaways

- **Dollar-cost averaging—investing a fixed amount at regular intervals—removes the emotional pressure of market timing and can lower your average cost per share over time.**

- **Research shows lump-sum investing often delivers higher returns, but DCA wins if it keeps you invested when fear would otherwise push you to cash.**

- **Automate your DCA plan through payroll deductions or recurring transfers to eliminate the risk of skipping investments during scary markets.**

- **DCA works best with diversified, low-cost index funds over long time horizons (10+ years) and is the natural approach for investing from regular income.**

- **The biggest risk isn't buying at the wrong time—it's waiting for the "perfect" moment that never comes and staying in cash while inflation erodes your purchasing power.**
