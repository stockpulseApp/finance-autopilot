# House Hacking Explained: How First-Time Buyers Are Cutting Housing Costs in Half

The dream of homeownership is colliding hard with economic reality. A [CNBC report](https://www.cnbc.com/make-it/2026/10/07/renters-homeownership-affordability.html) found 34% of U.S. renters say they may never afford a home. Meanwhile, first-time buyers made up just 21% of all home purchases in 2025—the lowest share since data collection began in 1981.

But while traditional homebuying feels increasingly out of reach, a growing cohort of first-timers is turning the equation on its head. They're not waiting for prices to fall or rates to drop. Instead, they're using a strategy called "house hacking" to make the math work today.

House hacking means buying a property, living in part of it, and renting out the rest to offset—or even eliminate—your monthly housing costs. It's landlording from day one, which isn't for everyone. But for buyers willing to trade some privacy and convenience for financial acceleration, it can be the difference between renting forever and building equity now.

This isn't a get-rich-quick scheme. It's a documented, financing-friendly strategy that the FHA, VA, and conventional lenders explicitly accommodate. If you can stomach being a live-in landlord for a few years, house hacking offers a concrete path to homeownership that doesn't require a six-figure down payment or a gift from family.

## What house hacking actually looks like

At its core, house hacking is simple: you occupy one unit of a property while collecting rent from the others. The rental income covers part (or all) of your mortgage, insurance, taxes, and maintenance.

Common formats include:

**Duplex, triplex, or fourplex:** You live in one unit and rent the others. A fourplex is the sweet spot because it's the largest property that still qualifies for owner-occupied financing (FHA, VA, conventional). Anything with five or more units requires a commercial loan with a much larger down payment.

**Single-family home with roommates:** You rent out bedrooms to housemates. Less passive income than a multifamily, but easier to find in suburban or single-family-zoned neighborhoods.

**Accessory dwelling unit (ADU):** You live in the main house and rent out a separate structure on the property—a basement apartment, converted garage, or backyard cottage. Local zoning and ADU laws vary widely, so research your city's rules first.

**Short-term rental hybrid:** You rent part of the property on Airbnb or Vrbo while occupying the rest. This can generate higher income but comes with more management headaches and regulatory risk (many cities restrict short-term rentals).

[BiggerPockets](https://www.biggerpockets.com/blog/house-hacking), the real estate investor community that popularized the term, offers comprehensive resources on choosing and executing each approach. The platform notes that house hacking works best for buyers who "prioritize wealth-building over lifestyle perfection" and are comfortable with delayed gratification.

## The money: a worked example

Let's run the numbers on a realistic scenario.

**Meet Jordan Chen,** 28, a product manager in Nashville earning $78,000 a year. Jordan has saved $18,000 for a down payment and closing costs. She's been renting a one-bedroom apartment for $1,650/month and watching her friends buy houses with help from their parents. She doesn't have that option.

Jordan finds a duplex listed at $340,000 in a walkable neighborhood. Each unit is a two-bedroom, one-bath layout. The seller is an investor who's owned it for 15 years and kept rents below market.

**Her financing:**
- Purchase price: $340,000
- Down payment (FHA 3.5%): $11,900
- Closing costs (≈3%): $10,200
- Total cash needed: $22,100

She uses $18,000 of her savings and borrows $4,100 from a 401(k) loan at her employer (not ideal, but it gets her in the door).

**Her monthly costs:**
- Mortgage (30-year FHA at 6.75%): $2,140
- Property tax: $400
- Insurance: $180
- HOA/maintenance reserve: $150
- **Total: $2,870/month**

**Her rental income:**
Jordan lives in Unit A and rents Unit B to a couple for $1,600/month (market rate for the area). She's conservative and budgets for one month of vacancy per year.

- Annual rent collected: $17,600
- Monthly average: $1,467

**Her net housing cost:**
$2,870 (total expenses) - $1,467 (rental income) = **$1,403/month**

That's $247/month *less* than her old apartment, and now she's building equity. Over 12 months, she'll pay down roughly $4,200 in principal and capture any home appreciation. If Nashville home values rise 3% (the long-term average), her equity position improves by another $10,200.

After two years, Jordan refinances out of the FHA loan to drop mortgage insurance, reducing her payment by $150/month. She raises rent to $1,750 to keep pace with market increases. Her out-of-pocket cost drops to $970/month.

At year three, she's saved enough to buy a single-family home for herself and turns the duplex into a fully rented investment property. Both units now generate $3,500/month in rent against her $2,870 in expenses—a $630/month positive cash flow that covers part of her new mortgage.

This is the house hacking playbook in action.

## Financing: how to qualify

One of house hacking's biggest advantages is access to owner-occupied loan programs. Because you're living in the property, you can use low-down-payment, government-backed mortgages that wouldn't be available for a pure investment property.

**FHA loans:** 3.5% down for properties with up to four units. You must live in one unit as your primary residence for at least 12 months. Credit score minimums are typically around 580, though many lenders prefer 620+. The catch: you'll pay mortgage insurance (PMI) for the life of the loan unless you refinance.

**VA loans:** If you're a qualifying veteran or active-duty service member, you can buy a multifamily property (up to four units) with zero down. No PMI. This is the most powerful house hacking tool available, but eligibility is limited.

**Conventional loans:** 5% down for a duplex, 10% for a triplex, 15% for a fourplex (some lenders offer 10% across the board). Better rates than FHA if your credit is strong (700+), and PMI drops off automatically once you hit 78% loan-to-value.

**Using rental income to qualify:** Here's where it gets interesting. Lenders will count 75% of projected rental income toward your qualifying income, even if you don't have a rental history yet. You'll need a signed lease or an appraisal showing market rents, but this can dramatically increase your borrowing power.

[Lower Mortgage](https://www.lower.com/mortgages/what-is-house-hacking) explains that for FHA financing on a multifamily property, lenders typically use rental income from the vacant units to help you qualify—assuming you can document market rents with an appraisal.

A [mortgage expert on Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/TheShawnKaplan/videos/comment-househack-your-first-home-doesnt-have-to-be-your-dream-home-it-can-be-th/1669198258257215) emphasizes that buying a first home isn't impossible for beginners—the key is preparation and understanding your financing options.

**Debt-to-income ratio matters:** Lenders want your total monthly debt payments (including the new mortgage) to be no more than 43–50% of your gross income. Rental income helps offset the mortgage in this calculation, which is why a fourplex borrower might qualify for a larger loan than a single-family buyer with the same income.

## The risks and headaches

House hacking isn't passive income. You're a landlord, and landlording is work.

**Tenant issues:** Screening tenants, enforcing leases, handling maintenance requests, and dealing with late rent or evictions are now your problem. If you live next door to a problem tenant, you can't ignore it.

**Vacancy risk:** If a unit sits empty for three months, you're covering the full mortgage out of pocket. Budget a cash reserve of at least three to six months of total expenses before you buy.

**Unexpected repairs:** When the water heater dies or the roof leaks, you're responsible. First-year ownership often brings surprise costs—home inspections don't catch everything. Set aside 1% of the property value annually for maintenance.

**Privacy trade-offs:** You're sharing walls, driveways, or common spaces with people who are paying you rent. The power dynamic is awkward. Your home stops feeling like a sanctuary when you're managing the property.

**Occupancy requirements:** FHA and VA loans require you to move in within 60 days of closing and live there for at least 12 months. You can't change your mind and turn it into a pure rental immediately without violating your loan terms.

**Appreciation isn't guaranteed:** Real estate is cyclical. If you buy at the peak and the market corrects, you could end up underwater. Don't assume 5% annual appreciation—plan for flat or negative years.

**Financing complexity:** Lenders scrutinize multifamily loans more carefully than single-family purchases. Expect more paperwork, stricter appraisals, and longer timelines. If the appraisal comes in low, the deal could fall apart.

A [social media post by Myia Butler](https://www.instagram.com/reel/DeR3DzXg0EQ) warns first-time buyers against common mistakes and highlights house hacking as a strategy worth exploring—but only if you're prepared for what it entails.

## Tax and legal considerations

Rental income is taxable, but so are your expenses—and the tax code is generous to landlords.

**Deductible expenses include:**
- Mortgage interest (on the rental portion of the property)
- Property taxes (rental portion)
- Insurance (rental portion)
- Repairs and maintenance
- Utilities you pay for tenants
- Property management fees (if you hire help)
- Depreciation (this is a big one—you can depreciate the building over 27.5 years, creating a paper loss that offsets rental income)

If you live in a duplex and rent half, you allocate 50% of most expenses to the rental side. A CPA experienced in real estate will help you maximize deductions without triggering an audit.

**The home sale exclusion gets tricky:** When you eventually sell, the IRS lets you exclude up to $250,000 in capital gains ($500,000 for married couples) if you've lived in the home for two of the past five years. But if part of the property was rented, you may owe depreciation recapture tax on that portion. Consult a tax professional before you sell.

**Landlord-tenant law:** Every state has different rules on security deposits, eviction procedures, habitability standards, and lease requirements. Violating these laws can cost you thousands in fines or legal fees. Read your state's landlord-tenant statute or hire a local attorney to draft your lease.

**Insurance:** Standard homeowners insurance won't cover rental activity. You need a landlord policy (or a "dwelling fire" policy with liability coverage). Expect to pay 20–30% more than a standard homeowners premium.

## Finding the right property

Not every multifamily listing is a good house hack. Here's what to prioritize:

**Strong rental demand:** Look for neighborhoods near universities, hospitals, downtown job centers, or public transit. Vacancy rates should be low (<5% is ideal). Check Craigslist, Zillow Rentals, and local Facebook groups to see how quickly similar units are renting.

**Comparable rents:** Run the numbers before you make an offer. If similar duplexes in the area rent for $1,500/unit and the seller is asking a price that would require $2,000/unit to break even, walk away.

**Condition and deferred maintenance:** Older buildings can be goldmines or money pits. Get a thorough inspection. Budget for immediate repairs (roof, HVAC, plumbing, electrical) before you assume rental income will cover everything.

**Separate utilities:** Properties where each unit has its own electric, gas, and water meters make life easier. You don't want to pay your tenant's heating bill.

**Unit mix:** Two-bedroom units rent faster and to more stable tenants than studios. One-bedroom units are a middle ground. Consider your target renter (young professionals, small families, grad students) and what they need.

**Owner-occupant appeal:** Buy something you'd actually want to live in for 12–24 months. If it's a dump, you'll be miserable even if the cash flow is great.

**Zoning and legal status:** Make sure the property is legally zoned for multifamily use and that any ADUs or basement apartments have proper permits. Unpermitted units can't be insured or financed, and the city can force you to stop renting them.

[NerdWallet](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/5-ways-to-invest-in-real-estate) notes that David Meyer, head of real estate investing at BiggerPockets, highlights house hacking as a strategy that allows investors to buy up to four units and still qualify for owner-occupied financing—a critical advantage.

## Managing tenants: DIY or hire out?

You have two options: self-manage or hire a property management company.

**Self-managing saves money** (management fees typically run 8–12% of monthly rent) but costs time. You're on call for emergencies, you're screening applicants, you're collecting rent and enforcing late fees, you're coordinating repairs.

**Hiring a manager** makes sense if you travel frequently for work, hate confrontation, or plan to move out and keep the property as a pure rental. The trade-off: lower cash flow.

For a first house hack, most owners self-manage. You're living on-site, so response time is instant. Use this as a learning experience. If you scale up to more properties later, you'll understand what good management looks like.

**Tenant screening tips:**
- Run a credit check and background check (services like Cozy or TurboTenant make this easy)
- Verify employment and income (ask for pay stubs or a W-2)
- Call previous landlords (not just the current one—they might lie to get rid of a problem tenant)
- Meet in person and trust your gut

## When house hacking makes sense (and when it doesn't)

**House hacking is a strong fit if you:**
- Have a stable job and decent credit (620+ FICO)
- Can save $10,000–$25,000 for down payment and reserves
- Are comfortable living with tenants or in close proximity to them
- Want to build equity faster than traditional renting or homebuying
- Are willing to learn basic landlording skills
- Plan to stay in the area for at least 3–5 years
- Can handle maintenance issues and tenant complaints

**Skip house hacking if you:**
- Value privacy and personal space above all else
- Have unpredictable income or job stability issues
- Don't have an emergency fund to cover vacancies or repairs
- Hate confrontation or managing people
- Are buying in a market with weak rental demand
- Plan to move in 1–2 years (transaction costs will eat your gains)

## After the house hack: next steps

Most house hackers don't live with tenants forever. The typical path:

**Year 1–2:** Live in one unit, rent the others, learn the ropes, build equity.

**Year 3–4:** Buy a second property (another house hack or a single-family home for yourself). Move out of the first property and turn it into a fully rented investment. If you kept the FHA or VA loan, you can now qualify for another owner-occupied loan on the new property.

**Year 5+:** Repeat. Some investors scale to 5–10 properties this way, using equity from earlier purchases to fund down payments on new ones. Others cash out, pocket the gains, and move on to other investments.

The key advantage: you're building a rental portfolio without starting as a full-time investor. You're learning property management, forced savings through mortgage paydown, and benefiting from leverage (the bank's money working for you).

## Key takeaways

- **House hacking means living in one unit of a property while renting out the rest** to offset your mortgage, taxes, insurance, and maintenance costs—potentially eliminating your housing expense entirely.

- **Owner-occupied financing (FHA, VA, conventional) lets you buy a multifamily property with as little as 3.5% down** and use projected rental income to help you qualify, a massive advantage over investment property loans.

- **Expect to self-manage tenants, handle repairs, and sacrifice privacy** in exchange for faster equity building and reduced monthly costs—this isn't passive income, it's active landlording.

- **Run conservative numbers before you buy**: budget for vacancies, maintenance, and surprise expenses, and make sure comparable rents in the area support your projected cash flow.

- **Tax advantages (depreciation, expense deductions) and forced savings (mortgage paydown) can accelerate wealth building**, but consult a CPA to structure everything correctly and avoid costly mistakes at sale time.
