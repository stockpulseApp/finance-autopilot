# House Hacking Explained for First-Time Buyers

You're scrolling through listings, watching your friends buy homes, and wondering how anyone affords a $2,400 mortgage on a median household income. Meanwhile, someone you went to college with just mentioned they're "living rent-free" in their own house. Your BS detector goes off—until they explain they bought a duplex, moved into one side, and their tenant's rent check covers the mortgage.

That's house hacking, and it's one of the few strategies that lets you build equity while dramatically cutting your housing costs. No trust fund required.

## What house hacking actually means

House hacking is living in a property you own while renting out part of it to offset your mortgage and expenses. The rented portion can be a separate unit in a duplex or triplex, a basement apartment, a spare bedroom, or even a converted garage. The income from your tenant(s) reduces—or in ideal cases, eliminates—what you personally pay to live there.

The term was [coined by BiggerPockets](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/5-ways-to-invest-in-real-estate), an online real estate investing community, but the strategy itself is as old as the two-flat apartment buildings that line streets in Chicago, Boston, and Cleveland. What's changed is access: low-down-payment mortgages now make house hacking feasible for first-time buyers who don't have $80,000 sitting in a brokerage account.

Here's why this matters in 2025: The median home price in the U.S. sits around $420,000. A 20% down payment would be $84,000—money most people in their twenties and thirties don't have. House hacking lets you use an FHA loan (3.5% down) or a conventional loan (as low as 3% down) to buy a property with up to four units, as long as you live in one of them. That same $420,000 property now requires just $14,700 to $12,600 down, and your tenants help you cover the monthly nut.

## The numbers: a real-world example

Meet Sophie Chen, 29, a marketing manager in Minneapolis earning $68,000 a year. She was paying $1,500/month for a one-bedroom apartment—$18,000 a year disappearing into her landlord's equity.

After six months of searching, Sophie bought a duplex in the Powderhorn neighborhood for $340,000. She put 3.5% down ($11,900) using an FHA loan, closed with about $16,000 total out of pocket (including closing costs and reserves). Her mortgage payment, property taxes, and insurance total $2,680/month.

She lives in the lower unit and rents the upper unit for $1,400/month. Her net housing cost: $1,280/month—$220 less than her old rent, except now she's building equity in a $340,000 asset. Over the first year, roughly $4,800 of her payments went toward principal. Her tenant paid $16,800 in rent. Sophie's true cost of housing, after accounting for equity gained? About $7,200 for the year, or $600/month.

That's the house hacking promise: live cheaper than renting while your tenant pays down your mortgage.

## Four common house hacking strategies

**1. Multi-family (duplex, triplex, fourplex)**  
You buy a property with 2–4 units, live in one, rent the others. This is the classic approach and works best with FHA or VA loans, which cap at four units. A [BiggerPockets analysis](https://www.biggerpockets.com/blog/real-estate-1182) notes that fourplexes in secondary markets often cash flow immediately, since three rental incomes can exceed your mortgage payment.

**2. Single-family with an accessory dwelling unit (ADU)**  
You live in the main house and rent a backyard cottage, finished basement, or garage apartment. This works in neighborhoods zoned for ADUs and appeals to buyers who want a single-family feel. The downside: construction or conversion costs can run $60,000–$150,000 if the ADU doesn't already exist.

**3. Renting by the room**  
Buy a three- or four-bedroom house, live in one room, rent the others to housemates. This maximizes income density—three tenants at $700/month each yields $2,100—but requires sharing common spaces. Best for extroverts who don't mind communal living.

**4. Short-term rental (Airbnb)**  
Rent out a spare room or separate unit on Airbnb while you occupy the property. Income can be 30–50% higher than long-term rent, but income is variable, management is hands-on, and some HOAs or municipalities restrict short-term rentals. [One investor on X](https://x.com/_JacobReeves_/status/1799216849168584733?lang=uk) reported earning over $3,200 in their first full month of Airbnb house hacking, eliminating their mortgage payment entirely.

## How financing works (and why it's easier than you think)

Traditional investment property loans require 20–25% down and treat the property as a pure business asset. House hacking flips the script: because you'll live there, you can use an owner-occupant loan with minimal down payment.

**FHA loans:** 3.5% down, up to four units, credit scores as low as 580 (though 620+ gets better rates). The catch is mortgage insurance: you'll pay 0.55–0.85% of the loan amount annually until you refinance or reach 20% equity. FHA also lets lenders count 75% of projected rental income toward your qualifying income—even if the unit is currently vacant.

[A buyer on BiggerPockets](https://www.biggerpockets.com/posts/7341575) explained it this way: "When the appraiser values the duplex, they also estimate what the other unit should rent for. FHA lets the lender take 75% of that number and count it toward your income, even if nobody's living there yet. So if the appraiser says the other side should rent for $1,400, about $1,050 a month goes toward you qualifying."

**Conventional loans (Fannie Mae, Freddie Mac):** 3–5% down for first-time buyers, up to four units. No upfront mortgage insurance premium like FHA, but you'll pay monthly PMI until you hit 20% equity. Conventional loans require stronger credit (typically 680+) and lower debt-to-income ratios, but the ongoing costs are often lower than FHA if you have good credit.

**VA loans (for veterans):** 0% down, no mortgage insurance, up to four units. If you qualify, this is the gold standard. A veteran in Seattle could buy a $600,000 fourplex with zero down and immediately rent three units for $5,400/month total, making their net housing cost negligible.

[NerdWallet points out](https://www.nerdwallet.com/mortgages/learn/know-buying-investment-property) that even with these advantages, you'll need cash on hand for upfront costs like earnest money deposits, inspections, and appraisals—typically $3,000–$6,000 before closing.

## What most people get wrong (and what can go sideways)

**Underestimating landlord duties**  
You are now responsible for maintenance, tenant screening, lease enforcement, and midnight calls about broken water heaters. If you're house hacking a duplex, your tenant is quite literally your neighbor. Poor screening or conflict-avoidance will make your life miserable. Budget $100–$200/month for maintenance reserves and read your state's landlord-tenant laws before you list the unit.

**Overpaying in hot markets**  
Appreciation is nice, but cash flow keeps you solvent. In a [YouTube explainer on house hacking](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=i-lXc8Tmv8A), investors emphasized the difference between cash flow and appreciation markets: "If you're in Seattle or San Francisco, you might not cash flow on day one, but you're betting on appreciation. If you're in Indianapolis or Cleveland, you might cash flow immediately but see slower appreciation." Neither is wrong, but you need to know which game you're playing.

**Ignoring vacancy and turnover costs**  
Your tenant will eventually move out. Figure 8–10% vacancy annually (one month empty per year), plus turnover costs: cleaning, minor repairs, possibly a month of advertising. If you're counting on rent to make your mortgage payment and have no cushion, one bad tenant or vacancy will wreck your finances.

**Forgetting the exit strategy**  
FHA and VA loans require you to live in the property for at least 12 months. After that, you can move out and keep it as a rental, but if you want to use another low-down-payment owner-occupant loan, you'll need to sell or refinance the first property into a conventional investment loan. Refinancing costs 2–4% of the loan amount in fees, so plan accordingly.

## Finding and analyzing deals

A good house hacking candidate has three traits: location near jobs and amenities (tenants want convenience), rent potential that covers 60–100% of your mortgage, and deferred maintenance you can tackle over time without breaking the bank.

[CrossCountry Mortgage recommends](https://crosscountrymortgage.com/mortgage/resources/what-is-house-hacking) starting with rental comps: search Zillow, Apartments.com, and Facebook Marketplace for similar units in your target neighborhood. If two-bedroom units rent for $1,300/month and your mortgage would be $2,200, you're looking at a $900/month net cost—better than renting, but not a home run. If comparable units rent for $1,800 and your mortgage is $2,400, you're at $600/month—now we're talking.

Sophie, our Minneapolis buyer, used this formula:

- **Total monthly cost:** $2,680 (mortgage + taxes + insurance)  
- **Projected rent (75% for underwriting):** $1,400  
- **Net cost:** $1,280  
- **Comparison to renting:** Saving $220/month vs. her old apartment, plus building equity  

She also factored in a $150/month maintenance reserve and knew the duplex needed a new water heater within two years ($1,800). Her total first-year cash outlay: $16,000 to close, plus about $3,000 in repairs and vacancy. Still cheaper than paying rent with no equity.

## Tax benefits and long-term wealth building

You can deduct mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, repairs, and depreciation on the rental portion of your property. If 50% of your duplex is rented, you can deduct 50% of these expenses against your rental income, lowering your taxable income. Work with a CPA who understands real estate; this isn't TurboTax territory.

The real wealth, though, is forced savings. Every mortgage payment builds equity. Every year, your tenants pay down $4,000–$8,000 of your principal. After five years, you might have $40,000 in equity from principal paydown alone, plus any appreciation. You can then refinance, pull cash out, and buy another property—repeating the cycle.

[NerdWallet highlights](https://www.nerdwallet.com/article/mortgages/tips-for-first-time-home-buyers) that many people assume you need a ton of money to buy a home, but house hacking proves you can enter the market with less capital by turning your primary residence into an income-generating asset.

## Is house hacking right for you?

House hacking works best if you:

- Can commit to living in the property for at least a year  
- Are willing to manage tenants or pay a property manager (10% of rent)  
- Have stable income and $15,000–$25,000 saved for down payment and reserves  
- Aren't precious about your living situation for a few years  

It's not ideal if you:

- Travel constantly for work and can't respond to tenant issues  
- Have zero DIY skills and can't handle minor repairs  
- Want complete privacy and control over your living space  
- Are in a market where rent-to-mortgage ratios make the math impossible

## Key takeaways

- House hacking means buying a property, living in part of it, and renting the rest to offset your mortgage—often reducing housing costs below what you'd pay in rent.
- Low-down-payment loans (FHA at 3.5%, conventional at 3–5%, VA at 0%) make house hacking accessible to first-time buyers without massive savings.
- A typical house hack involves a duplex or triplex, with rental income covering 50–100% of the mortgage, depending on market and property.
- You'll need $15,000–$25,000 total for down payment, closing costs, and reserves, plus the willingness to be a landlord and handle tenant issues.
- The long-term wealth comes from forced equity (tenants pay your mortgage) and potential appreciation, letting you refinance or buy additional properties after a few years.
