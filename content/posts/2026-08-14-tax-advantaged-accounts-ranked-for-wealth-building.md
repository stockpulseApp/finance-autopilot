# Tax-Advantaged Accounts Ranked: The Real Wealth-Building Pecking Order

Most Americans leave serious money on the table. Not because they spend too much on lattes or fail to hustle hard enough, but because they funnel cash into the wrong tax-advantaged accounts—or skip them entirely.

The numbers tell the story: According to Vanguard's 2025 participant data, fewer than 14% of workers max out their 401(k) contributions, despite these accounts being one of the most accessible wealth-building tools available. Meanwhile, [Peter Thiel's Roth IRA reportedly grew to over $5 billion](https://wealthtender.com/insights/financial-planning/how-much-the-wealthy-keep-in-retirement-accounts) by legally investing in startup equity at pennies per share—representing more than 70% of his total net worth when disclosed. The gap between those who understand tax-advantaged account optimization and those who don't isn't just a rounding error; it's the difference between comfortable retirement and generational wealth.

Here's the framework nobody gives you: Not all tax-advantaged accounts are created equal. Some offer triple tax benefits. Others provide employer matches—literally free money. A few allow you to withdraw contributions penalty-free before retirement. And several let you pass wealth to heirs with enormous tax advantages.

This ranking cuts through the noise. We're comparing apples to apples across five dimensions: immediate tax benefit, growth potential, withdrawal flexibility, inheritance advantages, and contribution limits. Because the "best" account depends entirely on your income, timeline, and goals—but the hierarchy matters.

## The ranking methodology

We evaluated 11 major tax-advantaged account types using a weighted scoring system:

**Immediate tax benefit (25%):** Does the account reduce your taxable income today, or do you pay taxes upfront for tax-free withdrawals later?

**Growth potential (30%):** What's the combination of contribution limits, investment options, and compounding runway?

**Withdrawal flexibility (20%):** Can you access the money before retirement without penalties? Under what circumstances?

**Inheritance advantages (15%):** How does the account transfer to heirs, and what's the tax treatment for beneficiaries?

**Contribution limits (10%):** How much can you actually put in annually?

[Tax-advantaged accounts](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/t/tax-advantaged.asp) fall into two camps: tax-deferred (traditional 401(k)s and IRAs, where you deduct contributions now and pay taxes on withdrawals) and tax-exempt (Roth accounts, where you pay taxes upfront but withdraw tax-free). A third category—like Health Savings Accounts—offers both.

Each ranking includes real 2026 contribution limits and a worked example showing 30-year projections assuming 7% average annual returns (roughly the S&P 500's inflation-adjusted historical average).

## Tier 1: The untouchables

### 1. Health Savings Account (HSA)

**Score: 95/100**

The HSA is the only account in the U.S. tax code with triple tax advantages: tax-deductible contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses. After age 65, you can withdraw for any reason and pay only ordinary income tax (like a traditional IRA), but medical withdrawals remain tax-free for life.

**2026 limits:** $4,300 individual, $8,550 family, plus $1,000 catch-up if 50+

**Who wins:** High earners with high-deductible health plans who can afford to pay medical expenses out-of-pocket and let the HSA grow untouched for decades.

**Worked example:** Sarah, 35, maxes out her family HSA at $8,550 annually and invests it in a low-cost S&P 500 index fund. She pays current medical bills from checking. At 65, assuming 7% returns, her HSA holds roughly $861,000—all withdrawable tax-free for healthcare. Since healthcare costs average $315,000 per person in retirement (Fidelity 2025 estimates), she's covered with hundreds of thousands to spare.

**The catch:** You must have a qualifying high-deductible health plan (2026 minimum deductible: $1,650 individual, $3,300 family). Many employers don't offer one, or employees choose lower-deductible plans for peace of mind.

### 2. Roth 401(k) with employer match

**Score: 93/100**

If your employer offers a match, this is free money—full stop. The Roth 401(k) combines high contribution limits with tax-free growth and no required minimum distributions (RMDs) after the SECURE 2.0 Act.

**2026 limits:** $24,500 ($32,500 if 50+), plus employer match (doesn't count against your limit)

**Who wins:** Younger workers who expect higher tax rates in retirement, or anyone who wants tax diversification and maximal contribution room.

**Worked example:** Marcus, 30, earns $95,000 and contributes $24,500 to his Roth 401(k). His employer matches 50% of the first 6% of salary ($2,850). Total annual addition: $27,350. By 60, at 7% returns, he's sitting on $2.76 million—completely tax-free in retirement. Even better: he can pass this to heirs, who inherit a tax-free account (though they must withdraw over 10 years under current law).

**The catch:** Employer match dollars go into a *traditional* (pretax) 401(k), meaning those matched funds and their growth are taxable upon withdrawal. Also, high earners might prefer the immediate tax deduction of a traditional 401(k) if they're in the top bracket now but expect lower rates in retirement.

### 3. Traditional 401(k) with employer match

**Score: 91/100**

Same contribution limits and employer match as Roth 401(k), but you get the tax deduction today. For high earners in the 35% or 37% federal bracket, that's real money back immediately.

**2026 limits:** Same as Roth 401(k)

**Who wins:** Peak earners who expect to be in a lower tax bracket in retirement, or anyone maxing out contributions who needs the cash-flow relief of a smaller current tax bill.

**Worked example:** Jennifer, 45, earns $280,000 and maxes her traditional 401(k) at $32,500 (including catch-up). Her marginal federal rate is 35%, so she saves roughly $11,375 in federal tax this year. Her employer adds a $8,400 match. At 65, she'll have approximately $1.47 million (7% returns)—all taxable on withdrawal, but likely at lower rates if she keeps spending disciplined.

**The catch:** RMDs kick in at 73 (or 75 starting in 2033), forcing taxable withdrawals whether you need the cash or not. This can push retirees into higher brackets and increase Medicare Part B/D premiums.

## Tier 2: The heavy lifters

### 4. Roth IRA

**Score: 89/100**

The Roth IRA is the flexibility champion. Contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn anytime, tax- and penalty-free. No RMDs. Tax-free inheritance for beneficiaries (with a 10-year withdrawal clock). And you can invest in almost anything: stocks, bonds, REITs, even private placements if your custodian allows.

**2026 limits:** $7,500 ($8,500 if 50+), but income phaseouts start at $150,000 single / $236,000 married filing jointly

**Who wins:** Early-career professionals, anyone seeking tax diversification, or higher earners using the "backdoor Roth" strategy.

**Worked example:** Liam, 25, contributes $7,500 annually to a Roth IRA. By 65, he's deposited $300,000 and holds roughly $1.57 million at 7% returns—all tax-free. He can withdraw contributions before retirement for a home down payment or emergency. At death, his heirs get a tax-free inheritance (though they must empty the account within 10 years).

**The catch:** Income limits lock out high earners unless they use a backdoor Roth (contribute to a traditional IRA, then convert). Also, the contribution limit is tiny compared to 401(k)s.

### 5. Solo 401(k) / Individual 401(k)

**Score: 87/100**

For self-employed individuals and small business owners, the Solo 401(k) allows you to contribute as both employee and employer, effectively doubling contribution limits.

**2026 limits:** $24,500 employee contribution ($32,500 if 50+), plus up to 25% of net self-employment income as employer contribution. Total cap: $70,000 ($77,500 if 50+)

**Who wins:** Freelancers, consultants, and solo entrepreneurs with high income and no employees (other than a spouse).

**Worked example:** Nina, 40, runs a design consultancy netting $200,000. She contributes $24,500 as employee, plus $50,000 as employer (25% of net income after deducting half of self-employment tax), hitting the $70,000 max. Over 25 years at 7%, she accumulates $4.37 million.

**The catch:** You can't have full-time W-2 employees (part-timers under 1,000 hours/year are OK). Plan administration is more complex than an IRA.

### 6. SEP IRA

**Score: 83/100**

The Simplified Employee Pension (SEP) IRA is the Solo 401(k)'s simpler cousin: employer-only contributions, up to 25% of compensation.

**2026 limits:** Up to $70,000 or 25% of compensation, whichever is less

**Who wins:** Self-employed people who want simple setup, or small business owners who want to contribute for employees (though you must contribute the same percentage for all eligible workers).

**Worked example:** Omar, 50, is a self-employed consultant earning $150,000 net. He contributes $37,500 (25% of net earnings after adjustments). No catch-up contributions exist for SEPs, so he can't add the extra $8,000 available in Solo 401(k)s.

**The catch:** Employer-only contributions mean you can't "double dip" like a Solo 401(k). Also, if you have employees, you must fund their accounts proportionally—expensive if you have a team.

### 7. 529 College Savings Plan

**Score: 78/100**

The 529 isn't a retirement account, but for wealth-building families, it's a tax-advantaged powerhouse. Contributions grow tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified education expenses (including K-12 tuition up to $10,000/year) are federally tax-free.

**2026 limits:** No annual federal limit, but contributions over $19,000/year per beneficiary may trigger gift tax reporting (though you can superfund five years at once: $95,000). Lifetime limits vary by state (often $300,000–$500,000).

**Who wins:** Parents, grandparents, and high earners looking to reduce estate size while funding education.

**Worked example:** Grandparents contribute $95,000 (five-year superfunding) into a newborn's 529. At 7%, that grows to roughly $366,000 by college. Withdrawals for tuition, fees, room, and board are federally tax-free. Unused funds can now be rolled into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary (up to $35,000 lifetime, starting in 2024 under SECURE 2.0).

**The catch:** Non-qualified withdrawals face a 10% penalty plus income tax on earnings. Also, 529s can reduce financial aid eligibility, though less so than assets in the child's name.

## Tier 3: Situational power players

### 8. Traditional IRA

**Score: 74/100**

The original tax-deferred retirement account. Contributions are tax-deductible (if you meet income limits), growth is tax-deferred, and withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.

**2026 limits:** $7,500 ($8,500 if 50+), with deduction phaseouts starting at $79,000 single / $126,000 married (if covered by a workplace plan)

**Who wins:** People without access to a 401(k), or lower earners who benefit from the immediate deduction.

**Worked example:** Emma, 28, has no 401(k) and earns $65,000. She contributes $7,500 to a traditional IRA, cutting her taxable income and saving roughly $1,650 in taxes (22% bracket). By 68, her IRA holds about $1.63 million at 7%. All taxable on withdrawal, but she'll likely be in a lower bracket.

**The catch:** RMDs at 73+, income limits for deductions, and the same contribution cap as a Roth IRA—but without the flexibility or tax-free withdrawals.

### 9. Taxable brokerage account

**Score: 68/100**

Wait—a *taxable* account in a tax-advantaged ranking? Yes, because [the right taxable brokerage strategy offers surprising benefits](https://pacesferrywealth.com/tax-advantaged-savings-accounts-for-high-income-earners): no contribution limits, no withdrawal restrictions, preferential long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15%, or 20% vs. ordinary income rates up to 37%), and a step-up in basis for heirs (meaning they inherit assets at current market value, erasing all capital gains).

**Who wins:** High earners who've maxed tax-advantaged accounts, real estate investors, or anyone building wealth beyond retirement.

**Worked example:** Carlos, 38, maxes his 401(k) and Roth IRA, then invests $30,000/year in a taxable brokerage. He buys low-turnover index funds to minimize taxable distributions. After 30 years at 7%, he holds $2.83 million. If he sells in retirement, long-term gains are taxed at 15% (assuming income under ~$600,000 married). If he dies and leaves it to heirs, they get a step-up and owe $0 on prior gains.

**The catch:** No upfront tax break, and dividend/interest income is taxable annually. Tax-loss harvesting and asset location (e.g., holding dividend stocks in IRAs, growth stocks in taxable accounts) become critical.

### 10. ABLE Account

**Score: 62/100**

[ABLE accounts](https://www.empower.com/the-currency/money/investing-kids-account-options-how-start) are tax-advantaged savings for individuals with disabilities that began before age 26 (recently raised from 26 to 46 under proposed legislation). Contributions grow tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified disability expenses are tax-free.

**2026 limits:** $19,000/year

**Who wins:** Individuals with disabilities and their families, especially those who need to preserve means-tested benefits like Supplemental Security Income (SSI) or Medicaid.

**Worked example:** Jordan, 24, was diagnosed with cerebral palsy at birth. His family contributes $19,000 annually to an ABLE account. Funds cover adaptive technology, therapy, housing modifications—all tax-free. The account doesn't jeopardize his SSI benefits (unlike traditional savings over $2,000).

**The catch:** Narrow eligibility (disability onset before age 26), and balances above $100,000 can suspend SSI (but not Medicaid). Upon death, Medicaid may have a claim on remaining funds.

### 11. Trump Accounts (new as of 2026)

**Score: 58/100**

The Trump Account—a tax-deferred investment vehicle for children under 18—[just received proposed Treasury regulations](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/08/11/trump-accounts-employer-match-paycheck-contributions-treasury.html) allowing employers to match employee contributions or enabling pretax paycheck deductions for dependent children's accounts. It's essentially a kid-focused 401(k), with contributions deductible and growth tax-deferred until withdrawal (taxed at the child's rate, typically lower).

**2026 limits:** TBD in final regs, but early proposals suggest $5,000–$10,000/year per child

**Who wins:** Families with high incomes looking for generational wealth transfers and estate tax reduction, especially small business owners who can fund accounts as an employee benefit.

**Worked example:** Priya, 38, owns a small business and enrolls in her company's Trump Account program for her two kids, ages 8 and 10. She contributes $5,000 per child via pretax payroll deduction, and her business matches $2,500 per child. Over 10 years, each child's account grows to roughly $103,000 at 7%. At 25, they can withdraw for college, a home, or retirement—taxed at their (likely low) rate.

**The catch:** [Industry experts emphasize](https://www.investmentnews.com/retirement-planning/generational-wealth-strategies-are-shifting-as-families-and-business-owners-eye-trump-accounts/267805) Trump Accounts shouldn't be standalone—they're one piece of an estate plan alongside 529s, trusts, and custodial accounts. Regulations are still being finalized, and withdrawal rules/penalties for non-qualified distributions remain unclear.

## The asset location game

Choosing the *right* account matters. So does choosing the *right* investments for each account—a practice called asset location.

General rules (consult a CPA for your situation):

**Tax-deferred accounts (traditional 401(k), traditional IRA):** Hold high-yield bonds, REITs, or actively managed funds that generate lots of taxable income. You're already paying ordinary income tax on withdrawals, so you're not giving up preferential rates.

**Roth accounts (Roth 401(k), Roth IRA):** Hold your highest-growth assets—small-cap stocks, emerging markets, individual growth stocks. Let the biggest winners compound tax-free forever.

**HSAs:** Treat like a Roth for healthcare. Invest aggressively if you can cover medical costs out-of-pocket now.

**Taxable brokerage:** Hold tax-efficient index funds (low turnover, minimal distributions), municipal bonds (interest is federally tax-free), or individual stocks you'll hold long-term. Avoid high-yield bonds and actively managed funds that churn.

[According to Investopedia's tax-efficient investing guide](https://www.investopedia.com/articles/stocks/11/intro-tax-efficient-investing.asp), capital gains on investments held over one year are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%—far below ordinary income rates up to 37%. That spread is why asset location matters.

## How to prioritize: A decision tree

You can't max everything. Here's the priority ladder for most people:

1. **Contribute to 401(k) up to employer match.** Free money beats every other investment.

2. **Max HSA if eligible.** Triple tax advantage is unbeatable.

3. **Pay off high-interest debt (>7% APR).** Credit cards, personal loans—these are guaranteed "returns" that beat market risk.

4. **Build 3–6 month emergency fund in HYSA.** Liquidity matters.

5. **Max Roth IRA (or backdoor Roth if over income limits).**

6. **Return to 401(k) and max it out.**

7. **If self-employed: Solo 401(k) or SEP IRA to max allowable contributions.**

8. **Fund 529s for kids' education** (if applicable).

9. **Taxable brokerage for additional wealth-building.**

10. **Consider Trump Accounts or ABLE accounts if applicable.**

High earners might reorder based on current vs. expected future tax rates. A 28-year-old software engineer in the 24% bracket probably prioritizes Roth accounts. A 50-year-old executive in the 37% bracket might lean traditional for the immediate deduction, planning Roth conversions in early retirement.

## The inheritance angle

Wealth-building isn't just about you. How accounts transfer matters.

**Roth IRAs and Roth 401(k)s:** Heirs inherit tax-free (though non-spouse beneficiaries must withdraw within 10 years under SECURE Act rules). No step-up needed—already tax-free.

**Traditional IRAs and 401(k)s:** Heirs pay income tax on withdrawals at their rate. Ten-year withdrawal rule applies to non-spouse beneficiaries.

**Taxable brokerage accounts:** Step-up in basis at death. If you bought stock at $10 and it's worth $100 when you die, heirs inherit at $100 and owe $0 on that $90 gain if they sell immediately. Massive advantage.

**HSAs:** Spouse beneficiaries can treat it as their own HSA (triple tax advantage continues). Non-spouse heirs must close the account and pay income tax on the balance (like an IRA). Not ideal, but still better than many alternatives.

**529 plans:** Beneficiary can be changed to another family member. Unused funds can roll to a Roth IRA (up to $35,000 lifetime) under SECURE 2.0. Or withdraw and pay 10% penalty plus income tax on earnings.

For estate planning, high-net-worth families often combine strategies: max Roth accounts for tax-free inheritance, hold appreciating assets in taxable accounts for step-up, and use 529s and Trump Accounts to transfer wealth to younger generations while reducing estate size.

## When to talk to a pro

This ranking is a framework, not personalized advice. Consult a **Certified Financial Planner (CFP)** if:

- Your household income exceeds $250,000 and you're trying to optimize across multiple account types
- You're self-employed and deciding between Solo 401(k), SEP IRA, or defined benefit plans
- You're within 10 years of retirement and planning Roth conversions or managing RMDs
- You're handling an inheritance, windfall, or stock compensation (RSUs, ISOs, ESPP)

Talk to a **CPA or Enrolled Agent** if:

- You need state-specific tax guidance (many states don't follow federal Roth treatment)
- You're doing backdoor Roth conversions and need to navigate the pro-rata rule
- You have complex situations: multiple states, foreign income, business ownership, rental properties

Fee-only advisors (those who don't earn commissions) typically charge $2,000–$5,000 for a comprehensive plan, or 0.5%–1% of assets under management annually. For a $500,000 portfolio, that's $2,500–$5,000/year—potentially worth it if they save you $10,000+ in taxes or prevent a costly mistake.

## Key takeaways

- **HSAs offer triple tax advantages—deductible contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for healthcare—making them the single most powerful account for eligible savers.**

- **Employer 401(k) matches are free money; always contribute enough to capture the full match before funding other accounts.**

- **Roth accounts (Roth IRA, Roth 401(k)) provide unmatched flexibility and tax-free growth, especially valuable for younger workers and those expecting higher future tax rates.**

- **Asset location matters as much as asset allocation—put high-growth investments in Roth accounts, income-producing assets in tax-deferred accounts, and tax-efficient index funds in taxable brokerage accounts.**

- **Taxable brokerage accounts offer zero contribution limits and a step-up in basis for heirs, making them essential for wealth-building beyond retirement account caps.**
