# Tax-Advantaged Accounts Ranked: The Hierarchy of Wealth Building

Most Americans treat tax-advantaged accounts like a confusing buffet at a conference hotel — they know they're supposed to use them, but they grab the first thing that looks familiar and call it a day. That's expensive.

The difference between smart sequencing and random contributions can mean six figures over a working lifetime. Not because one account type magically returns 12% while another returns 8%, but because tax treatment compounds just like returns do.

This isn't about exotic offshore structures or things your accountant needs to explain three times. We're ranking the accounts Congress already gave you, in order of bang-for-buck if you're serious about building wealth. Some you've heard of. Some sound boring. One is criminally underused despite being the best deal in the tax code.

Let's be clear upfront: This is analysis, not personal advice. Your optimal sequence depends on income, health, employer match, state taxes, and a dozen other variables. If you're making six figures or have complex equity comp, hire a CFP and CPA who work together. If you're starting out or mid-career, this framework will point you in the right direction.

## The ranking: Where your next dollar should go

Here's the controversial part. Most advice treats retirement accounts as interchangeable flavors of the same thing. They're not. Each has distinct tax treatment, contribution limits, withdrawal rules, and strategic uses. Ranking them forces hard choices — which is exactly what you need when you have $500 or $5,000 a month to allocate.

**Tier 1: Employer 401(k) match (up to the match only)**

This isn't even an investment decision. It's a question of whether you accept free money. If your employer matches 4% and you contribute 3%, you're declining a raise.

Math: Contribute enough to get the full match. If your company matches 50% of the first 6% you contribute, put in 6% — that's an instant 50% return before the market does anything.

Meet **Priya**, 31, software engineer earning $115,000. Her employer matches dollar-for-dollar up to 5%. She contributes $5,750 annually; they add another $5,750. That match is a guaranteed 100% return, risk-free, vested after two years. No other account offers that.

Caution: *Only* contribute enough for the match at this stage. The 401(k) itself isn't the best wealth-building vehicle — the match is. We'll come back to additional 401(k) contributions later.

**Tier 2: Health Savings Account (HSA) — if eligible**

The HSA is the single best tax-advantaged account in the U.S. code, and roughly [70% of eligible Americans don't maximize it](https://useorigin.com/resources/blog/what-are-the-best-tax-advantaged-accounts). It's the only triple-tax-advantaged account: contributions are pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free forever.

2025 contribution limits: $4,300 individual, $8,550 family, plus $1,000 catch-up at 55+.

Here's what makes it unbeatable: After age 65, you can withdraw HSA funds for *any* reason and pay only ordinary income tax (like a traditional IRA), but medical withdrawals remain tax-free. Given that the average 65-year-old couple will spend [$315,000 on healthcare in retirement](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/investment-accounts), you have a massive tax-free spending category.

Strategy: Max the HSA, invest it aggressively (don't leave it in cash), and pay current medical expenses out-of-pocket if you can afford it. Save receipts. You can reimburse yourself tax-free decades later, letting the account compound in the meantime.

Eligibility catch: You must be enrolled in a high-deductible health plan (HDHP). For 2025, that means a deductible of at least $1,600 individual / $3,200 family. If your employer offers an HDHP option, run the numbers — the HSA tax benefits often outweigh the higher deductible.

**Tier 3: Roth IRA**

The Roth is the [workhorse of tax-free wealth building](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/tax-efficient-investing). You contribute after-tax dollars today, and every dollar of growth and every withdrawal after age 59½ is federally tax-free (and state tax-free in most states).

2025 limits: $7,000 annually ($8,000 if 50+). Income phaseouts start at $150,000 single / $236,000 married filing jointly.

Why Roth beats traditional IRA for most wealth-builders:

- **Tax arbitrage**: If you expect higher income in retirement (or higher tax rates generally), paying tax now locks in today's rate.
- **Flexibility**: You can withdraw contributions (not earnings) anytime, penalty-free. It's a stealth emergency fund.
- **No required minimum distributions (RMDs)**: Traditional IRAs force withdrawals at 73. Roth accounts don't, so wealth can compound longer.
- **Estate planning**: Inherited Roth IRAs are tax-free to beneficiaries, though they must be drawn down within 10 years under current law.

The Roth is especially powerful for people in their 20s through 40s. A 30-year-old who maxes a Roth IRA ($7,000 annually) from now until retirement at 65, assuming 8% average returns, will have roughly $1.4 million — entirely tax-free.

If you earn too much for direct Roth contributions, look into the "backdoor Roth" — contribute to a traditional IRA (non-deductible) and immediately convert to Roth. Legal, common, and [endorsed by the IRS](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1991519/000109690624000170/rvh_ex13.htm) through inaction.

**Tier 4: HSA and Roth IRA — the full max**

Once you've hit the employer match and opened an HSA and Roth, the next goal is maxing both. This should be the baseline for anyone serious about financial independence.

Combined 2025 max for a single 35-year-old: $4,300 (HSA) + $7,000 (Roth) = $11,300 per year. That's about $940/month, which is aggressive but achievable on a median professional salary if you control housing and car costs.

**Tier 5: Additional 401(k) or 403(b) contributions**

Now we return to the workplace retirement plan. Once you've maxed tax-free growth (HSA and Roth), the traditional 401(k) becomes attractive again for its high contribution limit and immediate tax deduction.

2025 limit: $23,500 ($31,000 with catch-up if 50+). Contributions are pre-tax (reducing taxable income today), and growth is tax-deferred until withdrawal in retirement, when you'll pay ordinary income tax.

This is where sequencing matters. Consider **Marcus**, 38, consultant earning $145,000. He gets the 4% match ($5,800), maxes his HSA ($4,300), and maxes his Roth IRA ($7,000). He still has $18,000/year he wants to invest. Putting that into his 401(k):

- Reduces his taxable income from $145,000 to $127,000
- Saves roughly $4,320 in federal tax (24% bracket) plus state tax
- Allows $18,000 to grow tax-deferred

The traditional 401(k) makes most sense when you're in a high tax bracket now and expect a lower bracket in retirement — the classic assumption for high earners. If that's not you, or if your 401(k) has terrible fund options (expense ratios above 0.5%), consider stopping after the match and moving to Tier 6.

One sleeper option: If your employer offers a **Roth 401(k)**, contributions are after-tax but grow tax-free like a Roth IRA, with no income limits. The $23,500 limit is shared between traditional and Roth 401(k) contributions. [Research from Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/investment-accounts) suggests blending traditional and Roth contributions can optimize tax efficiency across your working years.

**Tier 6: Mega Backdoor Roth (if available)**

This is for high earners whose employers offer after-tax 401(k) contributions and in-plan Roth conversions. It's not common, but if you have it, it's powerful.

How it works: The IRS limit for *total* 401(k) contributions (employee + employer + after-tax) is $70,000 in 2025. If you've maxed the $23,500 employee limit and received, say, $8,000 in employer match, you can potentially contribute up to $38,500 in after-tax dollars, then immediately convert those to Roth — either in-plan or by rolling to a Roth IRA.

This lets high earners funnel tens of thousands into tax-free Roth space annually. Check with your plan administrator; fewer than [20% of plans allow this](https://www.wsj.com/buyside/personal-finance/taxes/tax-advantaged-account), but tech and finance companies often do.

**Tier 7: Taxable brokerage account**

Once you've maxed all tax-advantaged space, congratulations — you're in the top few percent of savers. Additional money goes into a standard brokerage account.

Taxable accounts have no contribution limits, full liquidity, and no early withdrawal penalties. They're less tax-efficient, but [modern strategies like tax-loss harvesting and holding index funds long-term](https://www.nerdwallet.com/investing/learn/tax-efficient-investing) can minimize the drag. Long-term capital gains and qualified dividends are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%, depending on income — far better than the ordinary income rates you'd pay on traditional 401(k) withdrawals.

If you're building wealth past retirement accounts, prioritize low-turnover index funds and ETFs (VTI, VXUS, VOO) in taxable to minimize annual tax events.

**Tier 8: 529 education savings plan (if you have kids)**

Technically, 529s are outside the wealth-building hierarchy because they're earmarked for education. But if you have kids and you've handled Tiers 1–5, a 529 is a powerful tool.

Contributions grow tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified education expenses are federally tax-free. Many states offer tax deductions or credits for contributions. [The 2026 rules expand K–12 usage to $20,000/year](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/trading-investing/investment-accounts), and starting in 2024, unused 529 funds can be rolled into a Roth IRA for the beneficiary (subject to limits).

Most important: You control the account, not the child. If they don't go to college, you can change beneficiaries to another family member or yourself for grad school.

## The wealth-building hierarchy in action: A case study

Let's put this into practice with **Jordan**, 34, senior accountant earning $92,000 in a moderate cost-of-living city. Married, no kids yet, spouse earns $68,000.

**Current situation:** Jordan contributes 3% to his 401(k) (his employer matches 3%), has $8,000 in savings, and invests $200/month in a Robinhood account "for fun." Combined household income: $160,000.

**Optimized sequence:**

1. **401(k) match (both spouses):** Jordan increases to 3% ($2,760/year), spouse contributes 3% ($2,040/year). Combined employer match: $4,800/year free money.

2. **HSA (family plan):** They switch to the HDHP option at Jordan's employer, max the family HSA at $8,550/year. They invest it in a target-date fund and pay small medical bills out-of-pocket, saving receipts.

3. **Roth IRA (both spouses):** Max both at $7,000 each = $14,000/year. They're under the income phaseout, so direct contributions work.

4. **Additional 401(k):** Jordan has budget left and contributes an additional $500/month ($6,000/year) to his 401(k) to reduce taxable income.

**Total annual investing:** $35,350 ($4,800 match + $8,550 HSA + $14,000 Roth + $2,760 + $2,040 + $6,000 401(k)). That's 22% of gross income — ambitious but achievable by cutting car payments and limiting lifestyle inflation after a recent raise.

**Tax impact:** The $8,550 HSA and $8,760 in traditional 401(k) contributions reduce their taxable income by $17,310, saving roughly $3,800 in federal tax. The effective cost of $35,350 in contributions is really $31,550 after tax savings.

In 25 years at 7% real return, this strategy compounds to roughly $2.4 million. If they'd kept the scattered approach (3% to 401(k), $200/month to taxable), they'd have around $950,000 — a $1.45 million difference from sequencing alone.

## The tax-advantaged account flowchart

Here's the decision tree for your next dollar:

1. **Employed with a match?** → Contribute enough to get full match (usually 3–6%).
2. **Eligible for HSA?** → Max it ($4,300 individual / $8,550 family).
3. **Income under Roth IRA limit?** → Max Roth ($7,000 / $8,000 if 50+).
4. **Still have money to invest?** → Return to 401(k), contribute up to $23,500 total.
5. **Maxed 401(k)?** → Check for mega backdoor Roth option; if not, open taxable brokerage.
6. **Have kids?** → Fund 529 after securing your own retirement (oxygen mask principle).

If you're self-employed, the hierarchy changes slightly: Solo 401(k)s allow much higher contributions (up to $70,000 in 2025 including profit-sharing), and SEP IRAs offer simplicity for solo operators. [Some strategies combine Solo 401(k) with HSA and Roth for maximum tax efficiency](https://www.facebook.com/andrewcordlemoneyis/posts/the-wealthy-use-5-free-tax-accounts-to-build-tax-free-wealth-solo-401k-roth-ira-/1683724053312259).

## Common mistakes that cost five figures

**Mistake 1: Prioritizing taxable brokerage over tax-advantaged.**

Some people love the "no restrictions" appeal of Robinhood or Fidelity brokerage accounts. Fine for money you might need in five years. Disaster if it's retirement money. A $10,000 investment in a taxable account growing at 8% for 30 years, with annual tax drag of 1.5%, nets about $72,000. The same $10,000 in a Roth IRA becomes $100,000, tax-free. That's $28,000 lost to unnecessary taxes.

**Mistake 2: Leaving the HSA in cash.**

Most HSA providers default to a savings account earning 0.5%. That's a checking account with a fancy name. [Fidelity's HSA](https://www.nerdwallet.com/retirement/best/ira-accounts), for instance, lets you invest in the same funds as an IRA once you hit a small cash threshold. Invest it. You have decades.

**Mistake 3: Roth vs. traditional paralysis.**

People agonize over this. Here's a heuristic: If you're early career (under $80k income) or expect higher income later, Roth. If you're peak earnings years in the 24%+ federal bracket, traditional 401(k) contributions save more tax today. If unsure, split the difference — some Roth, some traditional. Diversifying your tax treatment is a hedge against future tax law changes.

**Mistake 4: Ignoring your 401(k) fund menu.**

The account is only as good as the investments inside. If your 401(k) only offers actively managed funds with 1.2% expense ratios, the tax deferral benefit is partially eaten by fees. [SEI research on tax-smart portfolios](https://www.seic.com/about-sei/thought-leadership/podcast-building-tax-smart-portfolios) emphasizes that manager selection and placement matter — high-turnover active funds are especially destructive in taxable accounts, but even in a 401(k), fees compound against you. Lobby your HR department for low-cost index options, or after maxing your match, prioritize Roth IRA and taxable over more bad 401(k) contributions.

**Mistake 5: Forgetting about state taxes.**

If you live in California or New York (high state income tax) and plan to retire in Florida or Texas (no state income tax), traditional 401(k) contributions save state tax today that you'll never pay in retirement. If you're retiring in the same high-tax state, Roth looks better. This is worth $20,000+ over a career.

## The psychological edge of forced sequencing

Ranking accounts does something beyond math: it creates friction that prevents lifestyle inflation. When your $8,000 bonus hits in March, the question isn't "What do I want?" but "Have I maxed my HSA and Roth this year?" That reframe is worth as much as the tax savings.

Automation helps. Most payroll systems let you split direct deposit. Route dollars to HSA and 401(k) before they hit checking. Set up automatic monthly transfers to your Roth IRA on the 1st of each month. Make wealth-building invisible.

One caution: Don't optimize so hard you create a cash crisis. Keep 3–6 months of expenses in a boring high-yield savings account (currently 4–5% at most online banks) before you chase tax efficiency. The worst financial decision is pulling money from a Roth IRA early or taking a 401(k) loan because you over-invested and couldn't cover your car repair.

## When to break the hierarchy

Rules are useful until they're not. A few situations where the ranking bends:

- **You're over the Roth IRA income limit and your 401(k) is excellent:** Max the 401(k) first, then do a backdoor Roth. The order matters less than the total.

- **You're saving for a house in 3–5 years:** Don't lock that money in retirement accounts. Use a taxable brokerage or high-yield savings. Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) can be withdrawn penalty-free, so some people use Roths as pseudo-house-fund accounts, but it's risky if the market drops when you need the money.

- **You're an ultra-high earner (say, $500k+):** You're likely phased out of Roth IRAs, and your tax bracket is 35% or 37% federal. Traditional 401(k) and backdoor Roth mega conversions dominate. Hire a CFP who specializes in equity comp and multi-state tax issues.

- **You have crushing high-interest debt:** Kill credit card balances (18%+ interest) before contributing beyond the 401(k) match. A guaranteed 18% return (via avoided interest) beats uncertain market returns.

## The 30-year horizon: What this strategy builds

Let's model the ranked strategy versus a random approach over 30 years for a household earning $150,000 combined.

**Scenario A (ranked sequence):** Max employer match ($7,500/year including match), max HSA ($8,550), max two Roth IRAs ($14,000), add $10,000 to 401(k). Total: $40,050/year. Assume 7% real return.

**Scenario B (random, common approach):** Contribute 5% to 401(k) with 3% match ($12,000/year total), save $5,000/year in taxable brokerage. Total: $17,000/year, same 7% real return but 1.2% annual tax drag in taxable account.

**After 30 years:**

- Scenario A: ~$4.0 million across accounts. $1.8M tax-free (Roth + HSA), $2.2M tax-deferred (401(k)).
- Scenario B: ~$1.6 million. Scenario A builds **$2.4 million more wealth** — a 150% improvement — just from sequencing and using the right accounts, with the same household earnings.

That's a different life. That's retiring at 58 instead of 68. That's funding your kids' weddings without stress. That's donating to causes you care about while you're alive to see the impact.

## Key takeaways

- **Employer 401(k) match is step one:** Free money beats every other strategy. Contribute enough to get the full match before anything else.

- **HSA is the king of tax efficiency:** Triple tax advantage (deductible, tax-free growth, tax-free withdrawals for medical) makes it the best wealth-building account for those eligible. Max it early and invest aggressively.

- **Roth IRA is the tax-free workhorse:** After the match and HSA, maxing a Roth IRA ($7,000/year) should be your next priority. Tax-free growth and withdrawals in retirement are unbeatable for long-term wealth.

- **Sequence matters more than you think:** The difference between random contributions and strategic sequencing can be $1.5 million or more over a 30-year career — same income, same total contributions, radically different outcomes.

- **Automate and revisit annually:** Set up automatic contributions in January, then review every April when limits and income change. Small tweaks compound. If your financial picture is complex (equity comp, multi-state, $300k+ income), hire a CFP and CPA to optimize.
